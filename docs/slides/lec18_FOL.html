Objects and General Rules

First-order logic (FOL) describes objects and their relationships.

Instead of a separate rule for each person:

\text{HumanSocrates}\rightarrow\text{MortalSocrates}

write one rule about all people:

\forall x\,\bigl(\text{Human}(x)\rightarrow\text{Mortal}(x)\bigr).

The domain is the nonempty set of objects that variables range over. Here, choose all people.

Read the quantifier as “for every x.” Human and Mortal are predicates, whose arguments refer to people. The propositional atom HumanSocrates does not expose an object argument. A finite collection can be enumerated propositionally, but FOL states the general rule directly, including for people we have not named.

Keep this lecture to about 30-35 minutes, leaving time for Homework 3 and midterm questions. Spend most of that time on quantifier scope, matching, and the single clause-form example. Lectures 17 and 18 are excluded from the upcoming midterm, as stated in the schedule.

Terms and Predicates

A term refers to an object. An atomic formula makes a claim about objects.

Building block Example Meaning
Constant \text{Ada} A named person
Variable x A person whose value may vary
Function term \text{Mother}(\text{Ada}) An object: Ada’s mother
Predicate \text{Knows}(\text{Ada},x) A claim: Ada knows x

Connectives \neg,\land,\lor,\rightarrow,\leftrightarrow combine formulas as before.

Does \text{Mother}(\text{Ada}) itself have a truth value?

No. It denotes an object. \text{Human}(\text{Mother}(\text{Ada})) makes a truth-valued claim about that object.

The domain is people. In this simplified model, Mother assigns one person to every person. Functions are total and single-valued. A relation such as Advises may hold for several pairs or none, so it should generally be a predicate rather than a function. Different names need not denote different objects unless the model or premises say so.

A FOL interpretation specifies the domain, the objects named by constants, functions on that domain, and the relations represented by predicates. An assignment supplies values for free variables. A model of a closed KB makes every sentence in the KB true.

Universal and Existential Quantifiers

Domain: people. \text{Happy}(x) means “x is happy.”

Universal: \forall

\forall x\,\text{Happy}(x)

Every person is happy.

One unhappy person makes the statement false.

Existential: \exists

\exists x\,\text{Happy}(x)

At least one person is happy.

One happy person is a witness that makes it true.

The quantifier binds x in its scope. In \forall x\,(\text{Human}(x)\rightarrow\text{Mortal}(x)), the parentheses put both predicates in that scope.

Existence does not mean exactly one. A witness is an object that satisfies the quantified condition. In Happy(x) alone, x is free: we must assign it a value to evaluate the formula. A closed sentence has no free variables.

On a finite domain, universal quantification behaves like AND across all objects, while existential quantification behaves like OR. This is an intuition for evaluation, not a claim that all FOL domains are finite.

Quantifier Order

Domain: people. An arrow from x to y means \text{Knows}(x,y).

\forall x\,\exists y\,\text{Knows}(x,y)

Each person knows someone. The witness may depend on x.

\exists y\,\forall x\,\text{Knows}(x,y)

One person is known by everyone. The same witness works for all x.

The left world satisfies only the first statement. The right world satisfies both.

Both diagrams use the same three-person domain, Ada, Bo, and Cy, with no additional Knows pairs. The left and right columns within each diagram are source and target copies of the same objects. Bo knowing Bo is allowed: we have not imposed an irreflexivity condition. Knows is directed and need not be symmetric.

The right-hand statement entails the left-hand one on a nonempty domain. Swapping mixed quantifiers can change meaning, but swapping two adjacent universals, or two adjacent existentials, does not.

Translating “Every” and “Some”

Domain: people. \text{Student}(x) and \text{Advisor}(y) identify roles. \text{Advises}(y,x) means “y advises x.”

Every Student Has an Advisor

\forall x\,\bigl(\text{Student}(x)\rightarrow \exists y\,(\text{Advisor}(y)\land\text{Advises}(y,x))\bigr)

Some Student Has an Advisor

\exists x\,\exists y\,\bigl(\text{Student}(x)\land \text{Advisor}(y)\land\text{Advises}(y,x)\bigr)

Use implication to restrict a universal rule to students. Use AND to require an existential witness to be a student.

Ask for the second translation before revealing it. Under a universal quantifier, Student(x) AND … would claim that every person is a student. Under an existential quantifier, Student(x) implies … can be satisfied by a nonstudent, so it does not establish a student witness.

The first statement does not assert that any students exist. If there are no students, every implication in it is true. The second does assert the existence of at least one student and an advisor, who need not be a different person unless inequality is added.

Negating Quantifiers

Moving NOT inward flips the quantifier:

\neg\forall x\,P(x)\equiv\exists x\,\neg P(x) \neg\exists x\,P(x)\equiv\forall x\,\neg P(x)

“Not everyone knows someone.” Rewrite \neg\forall x\,\exists y\,\text{Knows}(x,y) with NOT next to the predicate.

\exists x\,\forall y\,\neg\text{Knows}(x,y)

There is a person who knows nobody.

“Not everyone” means at least one exception. It does not mean “nobody.”

P(x) here stands for any condition on x. Move the negation across one quantifier at a time. Across a compound condition, also apply De Morgan’s laws. This prepares students for Homework 3, Problem 3, without solving its specific formula.

Instantiation

Instantiation replaces a quantified variable with a term.

Universal Instantiation (UI)

\forall x\,\text{Human}(x)

Any particular instance follows:

\text{Human}(\text{Ada}).

The rule applies to every object, including Ada.

Existential Instantiation (EI)

\exists x\,\text{Happy}(x)

Give a witness a fresh name k:

\text{Happy}(k).

We have not learned whether that witness is Ada.

A fresh witness name adds no claim that it differs from all known people. It simply avoids assuming which person satisfies the existential.

UI is ordinary logical consequence. EI is a witness introduction in an extended vocabulary for proof or satisfiability reasoning. Happy(k) is not an unrestricted consequence about an arbitrary preinterpreted name k. In natural deduction, the fresh-name assumption has a local scope and must not leak into an unjustified conclusion.

For a standalone existential with no surrounding universal dependency, the fresh name is a Skolem constant. For a nested existential we may need a function, covered later. UI also permits suitable function terms. Avoid variable capture when applying a substitution under quantifiers.

Substitution and Unification

A substitution \theta=\{x\mapsto\text{Bo}\} means “replace x with Bo.”

\text{Knows}(\text{Ada},x)\theta=\text{Knows}(\text{Ada},\text{Bo})

Unification finds substitutions that make two expressions identical.

\text{Knows}(\text{Ada},x)\qquad\text{Knows}(y,\text{Bo})

Argument Required match Binding
First Ada with y y\mapsto\text{Ada}
Second x with Bo x\mapsto\text{Bo}

Apply \theta=\{y\mapsto\text{Ada},\ x\mapsto\text{Bo}\} to both expressions. Both become \text{Knows}(\text{Ada},\text{Bo}).

The postfix theta notation means apply the substitution to the whole expression, just as SUBST(theta, alpha) did in the previous materials. Bindings replace variables, not constant or predicate names. Explain each argument before revealing its binding. Constants are Ada and Bo; x and y are variables.

[Sources] - AIMA Chapter 9, unification and substitutions. - https://aima.cs.berkeley.edu/algorithms.pdf [/Sources]

Most General Unifiers and Failure

A most general unifier (MGU) matches expressions without unnecessary bindings.

R(x,f(y))\qquad R(\text{Ada},f(z))

An MGU is \{x\mapsto\text{Ada},\ y\mapsto z\}. It leaves z unrestricted. Here R is a predicate and f a unary function.

Expressions Why unification fails
P(\text{Ada}) and P(\text{Bo}) Different constant symbols cannot match syntactically
P(x) and P(f(x)) Occurs check: x occurs inside f(x)

The occurs check rejects x\mapsto f(x): ordinary first-order terms must be finite.

R is any binary predicate, P any unary predicate, and f a unary function symbol. An MGU allows every other unifier to be obtained by further substitution. For example, additionally setting z to Bo also unifies the pair but is more specific. Reversing y maps to z is an equivalent MGU up to variable renaming.

Syntactic unification does not infer equalities between distinct constant symbols even if a possible interpretation maps them to the same object. Equality reasoning is a separate extension. In the occurs-check case, substitution cannot turn the finite term assigned to x into exactly that same term with an extra f around it. This is about finite syntactic terms, not whether a semantic function could have a fixed point.

Generalized Modus Ponens

Generalized modus ponens uses matching facts to apply a quantified rule.

What we know Formula
Rule \forall x\,(\text{Human}(x)\rightarrow\text{Mortal}(x))
Fact \text{Human}(\text{Socrates})
  1. Match the premise using \theta=\{x\mapsto\text{Socrates}\}.
  1. Apply the same substitution to the conclusion:

\text{Mortal}(x)\theta=\text{Mortal}(\text{Socrates}).

With several premises joined by AND, all must match known facts under one consistent substitution.

This reuses the first slide’s rule. We match Human(x) to Human(Socrates), then carry x’s binding into the conclusion. If the rule required Human(x) AND Tired(x), Human(Socrates) and Tired(Ada) would not suffice unless there were additional knowledge identifying their subjects.

Skolem Constants and Functions

Skolemization replaces existential variables with fresh witness symbols.

Quantified statement Witness representation
\exists y\,\forall x\,\text{Knows}(x,y) \forall x\,\text{Knows}(x,k): one fresh constant k
\forall x\,\exists y\,\text{Knows}(x,y) \forall x\,\text{Knows}(x,f(x)): a fresh function f

The function’s arguments record the surrounding universal variables on which a witness may depend. Different inputs may still share a witness.

The diagram shows one possible interpretation of f for the left world from the quantifier-order slide. Skolemization does not compute this function or claim that the relation has a unique witness. It chooses one permissible witness per input when such witnesses exist.

The symbols must be new to the whole problem. Skolemization preserves satisfiability in an expanded vocabulary, not logical equivalence over a fixed interpretation of the new symbols. This is why it is suitable for refutation. For several surrounding universal variables, use f(x1,…,xn). A standalone existential has no such dependency, so a constant suffices.

[Sources] - AIMA Chapter 9, Skolemization. - https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs257/slides/Lecture_7.pdf [/Sources]

Clause Form for Resolution

Conjunctive normal form (CNF): AND of clauses, each an OR of literals. In FOL, atoms can contain terms, such as \text{Human}(x).

Step Operation
1 Remove \rightarrow and \leftrightarrow
2 Move NOT inward, including across quantifiers
3 Rename bound variables to avoid collisions
4 Skolemize existentials with the correct dependencies
5 Distribute OR over AND and list the clauses

All remaining variables are implicitly universal. Skolemization preserves satisfiability. The other formula rewrites preserve equivalence.

To rename bound variables apart, (forall x P(x)) AND (exists x Q(x)) can use x in the first part and y in the second. Also standardize variables in different clauses apart before resolving, so variables with the same spelling in independent clauses are not accidentally tied together.

Parentheses still matter. Quantifiers may be moved to a prefix after safe renaming, provided their scope and dependencies are preserved. In A OR forall x B, pulling forall x outward requires that x not be free in A. Dropping the written universal quantifier is a clause notation convention, not replacement by a single named object.

[Sources] - AIMA Chapter 9, first-order clause conversion. [/Sources]

One Rule, Two Clauses

Every student has an advisor. Abbreviate: S(x) = student, A(y) = advisor, T(y,x) = y advises x.

\forall x\,\bigl(S(x)\rightarrow\exists y\,(A(y)\land T(y,x))\bigr)

Step Result
Remove implication \forall x\,\exists y\,(\neg S(x)\lor(A(y)\land T(y,x)))
Skolemize y with f(x) \forall x\,(\neg S(x)\lor(A(f(x))\land T(f(x),x)))
Distribute OR over AND (\neg S(x)\lor A(f(x)))\land(\neg S(x)\lor T(f(x),x))

Two clauses, using the same f(x):

C_1=\{\neg S(x),A(f(x))\},\qquad C_2=\{\neg S(x),T(f(x),x)\}.

The original formula comes from the translation slide. The first row combines implication elimination with pulling the existential outward, valid because y is not free in S(x) and the domain is nonempty. No negations need pushing further inward, and x and y are already distinct.

Introduce f fresh for this example, independent of the earlier Knows example’s f. The last formula has implicit universal x. The clauses require one chosen individual f(x) both to be an advisor and to advise the student x. Using unrelated functions for the two occurrences would allow two different people and could weaken the original requirement. For a nonstudent, both clauses hold through NOT S(x).

Set notation uses an inner set for the OR of literals and an outer set for the AND of clauses. C1 and C2 name the two clauses, not new logical atoms.

Resolution with Unification

Resolve clauses whose opposite-sign atoms match under a substitution:

C_1=\neg\text{Human}(x)\lor\text{Mortal}(x), \qquad C_2=\text{Human}(\text{Socrates}).

  1. Unify \text{Human}(x) and \text{Human}(\text{Socrates}):

\theta=\{x\mapsto\text{Socrates}\}.

  1. Apply \theta to the whole clauses, then remove the complementary pair.

C_3=\text{Mortal}(\text{Socrates}).

The substitution affects the remaining literals too. Keep both parent clauses when adding the resolvent.

The negation signs differ, but the atoms Human(x) and Human(Socrates) unify. Do not try to unify a negated formula with a positive formula directly. The resolvent is the disjunction of the remaining substituted literals. Here only Mortal(Socrates) remains.

This example has one variable-bearing parent and one ground fact, so variable renaming between parents is unnecessary. In general, standardize parent clauses apart first. AIMA’s full FOL resolution procedure also includes factoring. We do not present this single example as a complete implementation of that procedure.

Refutation: A Complete Small Proof

Query: \text{Mortal}(\text{Socrates}). Add its negation as clause 3.

4. Parents 1 and 2, \theta=\{x\mapsto\text{Socrates}\}: derive \text{Mortal}(\text{Socrates}).

5. Parents 4 and 3, no binding needed: derive \bot, the empty clause.

The KB and negated query cannot both hold. Therefore \text{KB}\models\text{Mortal}(\text{Socrates}).

Animate the same proof as the preceding slide, now completing refutation. Initial clauses are 1: NOT Human(x) OR Mortal(x), 2: Human(Socrates), 3: NOT Mortal(Socrates). Clause 3 is a temporary assumption for refutation, not an extra fact asserted by the original KB. The diagram highlights the currently used parents and derived clause at each step.

The empty clause has zero literals and is always false. It is different from the empty clause set, a conjunction of zero clauses that is true. We use bottom here as in Lecture 17. The square symbol used in HW3 names the same empty clause.

Quick Check

Domain: people. \text{Knows}(x,y) means “x knows y.”

For \forall x\,\exists y\,\text{Knows}(x,y), may we replace y with one fixed constant k? Why?

In general, no. Different people may need different witnesses. Use a fresh function f(x).

Do P(x,x) and P(\text{Ada},\text{Bo}) unify?

No. The first position requires x\mapsto\text{Ada}; the second requires x\mapsto\text{Bo}. One substitution cannot satisfy both.

P is a binary predicate here. Ada and Bo are constant symbols. Pause for an answer before each reveal. These check witness dependency and consistent substitution, the main new skills needed for Homework 3.

Takeaways

  • State the domain and define every predicate.
  • Quantifier order determines whether a witness may depend on another variable.
  • Unification uses one consistent substitution across each expression.
  • Skolem terms record witness dependencies in clause form.
  • Refutation proves entailment by deriving the empty clause from the KB and negated query.
