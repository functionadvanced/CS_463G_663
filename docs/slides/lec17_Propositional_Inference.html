Can the Robot Conclude It May Move?

Let B = battery ready, C = corridor clear, and M = movement permitted.

The robot knows:

B, \qquad (B \land C) \rightarrow M

Must M be true? Must M be false? Or is it still undetermined?

Take a quick vote without revealing the answer yet. The intended answer is undetermined. We know B, but lack information about C and M. Do not read the implication as a biconditional. M is a proposition about permission, not an instruction that is executed automatically. The example will return at the end.

Facts, Rules, and a Query

A knowledge base (KB) is a collection of sentences assumed true.

Role Robot example
Fact B
Rule (B \land C) \rightarrow M
Query Does M follow from the KB?

We read all KB sentences together as a conjunction:

\text{KB} = B \land \bigl((B \land C) \rightarrow M\bigr)

Inference asks what follows from the sentences, not what seems plausible.

Clarify that rules and facts are both formulas. Writing KB as a set or as their conjunction is a representational choice. The assumed facts could be wrong about the physical world; inference guarantees a consequence of the encoded premises, not the correctness of the sensors.

[Sources]

  • AIMA Chapter 7, knowledge-based agents. Original robot encoding.

A Model Is a Compatible World

An assignment gives every proposition a truth value. It is a model of a formula when that formula evaluates to true.

P Q P \rightarrow Q Model of P \rightarrow Q?
T T T Yes
T F F No
F T T Yes
F F T Yes

The implication rules out exactly one world: P true and Q false.

T and F abbreviate true and false. Textbooks also call a full assignment a model and then say it satisfies or fails to satisfy a formula. Here, compatible world always means satisfying assignment. Ask students to identify the excluded row before explaining it.

[Sources]

  • https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/logic/propositional.html

Entailment Means Every Compatible World

\text{KB} \models \alpha

The query \alpha is true in every model of the KB.

Alpha names the query formula; it need not be a single atom. Explain the set inclusion visually. The reverse inclusion is not required: the query may hold in worlds that violate the KB.

  • Entailment definition: https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/logic/inference.html

Ignore Rows That Violate the KB

\text{KB} = P \land (P \rightarrow Q), \qquad \alpha = Q

P Q P \rightarrow Q KB true? Relevant to the query?
T T T Yes Yes: Q is true
T F F No No
F T T No No
F F T No No

\text{KB} \models Q. No row makes the KB true and the query false.

Have students find the rows where the complete KB is true, not merely where one rule is true. There is exactly one such row. This is a semantic verification of modus ponens, which we will name later.

One Counterexample Is Enough

Now reverse the proposed reasoning:

\text{KB} = Q \land (P \rightarrow Q), \qquad \alpha = P

Assignment KB true? Query P true?
P=\text{true},\ Q=\text{true} Yes Yes
P=\text{false},\ Q=\text{true} Yes No

\text{KB} \not\models P. The second row is a countermodel: a model of the KB that falsifies the query.

This is the fallacy of affirming the consequent. One supporting example never proves entailment, but one countermodel disproves it. The two listed assignments exhaust the KB’s models over P and Q.

[Sources]

  • Original countermodel example. AIMA Chapter 7, entailment.

Not Entailed Does Not Mean False

Return to \text{KB}=B\land((B\land C)\rightarrow M).

Both of these assignments satisfy it:

B C M Compatible with KB?
T F F Yes
T F T Yes

\text{KB} \not\models M \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{KB} \not\models \neg M

The truth of M is undetermined by the KB. Missing information is not automatically false.

These are two witnesses, not an exhaustive list: B=true, C=true, M=true is also a model. The three cases supported, refuted, and undetermined describe our information; they do not introduce a third truth value into classical propositional logic. A closed-world convention would be an additional assumption, not the default here.

Your Turn: Which Queries Follow?

A robot has two routes: A and B mean the respective route is open; G means the destination is reachable.

\text{KB} = (A \lor B) \land (A \rightarrow G) \land (B \rightarrow G)

Does the KB entail each of these queries?

G \qquad A \qquad \neg A

Justify each answer with all compatible worlds or a countermodel.

Allow three minutes of pair work. A and B are new symbols for this route example, not the earlier battery variables. Students may reason by cases rather than write all eight rows. Ask for explicit assignments when they claim non-entailment. Keep the answers on the next slide hidden until discussion ends.

The Destination Is Certain; the Route Is Not

The KB has exactly these three models over A,B,G:

A B G
T F T
F T T
T T T
  • G is entailed: every surviving world has G true.
  • A is not entailed: the second row is a countermodel.
  • \neg A is not entailed: the first row is a countermodel.

Reachability can be certain even when the open route is unknown.

Explain why G=false is impossible: the disjunction requires at least one open route, and its implication forces G=true. We will prove this same conclusion with resolution later, without listing all assignments.

Classify a Formula Across All Worlds

Classification Truth pattern Example
Valid / tautology True in every assignment P \lor \neg P
Unsatisfiable / contradiction True in no assignment P \land \neg P
Contingent True in some, false in others P \land Q

Satisfiable means true in at least one assignment.

Valid formulas and contingent formulas are both satisfiable.

Make explicit that valid/unsatisfiable/contingent form the three-way classification used in Homework 3, while satisfiable is a broader yes/no property. Validity concerns one formula across all assignments; entailment relates a KB to a query.

Quick Check: Which Kind of Formula?

Formula Classification
(P \land Q) \rightarrow P Valid
(P \lor Q) \land \neg P \land \neg Q Unsatisfiable
P \rightarrow Q Contingent

For the contingent formula, give one satisfying assignment and one falsifying assignment.

P=\text{false},Q=\text{false} satisfies it; P=\text{true},Q=\text{false} falsifies it.

Reveal one classification at a time. First row: whenever the premise is true, P is already true. Second: the disjunction conflicts with the last two conjuncts. Third: not every implication is a tautology. These are different formulas from the homework.

Entailment as a Satisfiability Question

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) asks whether any assignment satisfies a formula.

\text{KB} \models \alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{KB} \land \neg\alpha \text{ is unsatisfiable}

Test result for \text{KB}\land\neg\alpha Meaning
A satisfying assignment exists It is a countermodel to the query
No satisfying assignment exists The query is entailed

Try to make the premises true and the conclusion false. If that is impossible, the conclusion follows.

Read the equivalence in words before introducing SAT as a computational problem. This is the foundation of refutation. Do not equate satisfiable with entailed: the negated query is essential.

[Sources]

  • https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/logic/inference.html

First Check That the KB Is Consistent

Suppose sensor reports give:

\text{KB} = C \land \neg C

  • No assignment satisfies the KB.
  • In classical logic, an inconsistent KB entails every query.
  • This does not make every claim about the physical world true.

No compatible worlds means the premises need repair, not that the robot knows everything.

The universal condition for entailment holds vacuously over an empty set of models. In a practical pipeline, check the KB’s consistency before treating a yes answer as useful evidence. Other logics handle inconsistency differently, but this course example uses classical logic.

[Sources]

  • Consequence of the classical model-theoretic entailment definition. Original sensor example.

Model Checking Searches for a Countermodel

Conceptual pseudocode; model is a complete truth assignment.

def entails(kb, query):
    symbols = symbols_in(kb, query)
    for model in all_assignments(symbols):
        if evaluate(kb, model):
            if not evaluate(query, model):
                return False
    return True
  • Enumerate symbols from both the KB and the query.
  • Skip assignments that violate the KB.
  • n symbols give 2^n assignments before pruning.

Define all three helpers verbally: symbols_in gathers distinct proposition symbols, all_assignments enumerates Boolean assignments, evaluate applies the truth tables. The snippet is pseudocode, not a complete Python program. Step through the highlighted lines with the earlier reversed-implication countermodel. Ask why returning True inside the loop would be wrong. Formula evaluation adds work per assignment; do not claim O(2^n) regardless of formula size.

Meaning and Proof Are Different

Notation What it says
\text{KB}\models\alpha The query is true in every model of the KB
\text{KB}\vdash\alpha Our proof system derives the query

Sound: everything the proof system derives is entailed.

Complete: every entailed query can be derived by that system.

A sound proof cannot invent a consequence. A complete system can reach every consequence in its scope.

The single turnstile is a derivability relation; the double turnstile is semantic entailment. Completeness is relative to a logic and proof system, not a guarantee that any arbitrary short search will find the proof. If using the term algorithm, it must exhaust its complete procedure before a negative result is conclusive.

[Sources]

  • AIMA Chapter 7, inference and proofs.

Two Sound Inference Rules

Modus Ponens

P\rightarrow Q,\quad P

Therefore:

Q

Affirm the premise; conclude its consequence.

Modus Tollens

P\rightarrow Q,\quad \neg Q

Therefore:

\neg P

Deny the consequence; rule out its premise.

Neither rule lets us infer P from Q and P\rightarrow Q.

Link modus ponens to the earlier truth table and modus tollens to the implication’s one forbidden row. The Latin names are conventional names for these patterns. No new probability or causal assumption is involved.

[Sources]

  • AIMA Chapter 7, sound propositional inference rules.

CNF Gives Proofs a Uniform Input

Conjunctive normal form (CNF) is an AND of clauses.

(P\lor\neg Q)\land(Q\lor R)\land\neg R

Term Meaning Examples
Literal An atom or its negation P, \neg Q
Clause An OR of literals P\lor\neg Q
Unit clause A clause with one literal \neg R

All clauses must hold. Within a clause, at least one literal must hold.

Conjunctions and disjunctions can have more than two inputs. A unit clause is already valid CNF syntax; it does not need extra literals. Distinguish a formula from a set of clauses with implicit conjunction.

[Sources]

  • https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/logic/propositional.html

Rewrite Without Changing the Models

  1. Remove implications and biconditionals.

P\rightarrow Q\equiv\neg P\lor Q

P\leftrightarrow Q\equiv(\neg P\lor Q)\land(\neg Q\lor P)

  1. Move negation inward using De Morgan and double negation.
  2. Distribute OR over AND until the outer operation is AND.

X\lor(Y\land Z)\equiv(X\lor Y)\land(X\lor Z)

These propositional rewrites preserve equivalence over the same symbols. Later, FOL Skolemization preserves satisfiability rather than equivalence in the original language; do not introduce that machinery here. Naive distribution can increase formula size substantially; the small examples are for understanding the transformation.

[Sources]

  • AIMA Chapter 7, conversion to conjunctive normal form.

Worked Conversion: Distribute Carefully

Start with \neg(P\rightarrow Q)\lor R.

Remove the implication:

\neg(\neg P\lor Q)\lor R

Move negation inward:

(P\land\neg Q)\lor R

Distribute:

(P\lor R)\land(\neg Q\lor R)

Pause before each reveal and have students predict the next expression. The result contains two clauses, not P OR NOT Q OR R. For a quick check, set R=false and verify that both the original and the result require P=true and Q=false.

Your Turn: One Clause or Two?

Convert both formulas to CNF. Show the intermediate rewrite.

Formula 1

P\rightarrow(Q\land R)

One premise requires two consequences.

Formula 2

(P\land Q)\rightarrow R

Two conditions jointly require one consequence.

Why should moving a conjunction across the implication change the clause structure?

Allow three minutes to work in pairs. Ask students to eliminate the implication before deciding how many clauses they need. The two forms deliberately expose a common distribution and negation error.

CNF Answers: The Conjunction’s Location Matters

Formula 1

P\rightarrow(Q\land R) \equiv\neg P\lor(Q\land R)

\equiv(\neg P\lor Q)\land(\neg P\lor R)

Formula 2

(P\land Q)\rightarrow R \equiv\neg(P\land Q)\lor R

\equiv\neg P\lor\neg Q\lor R

The first answer has two clauses; the second has one. In the first, P=true forces Q and R. In the second, R is forced only if P and Q are both true. Ask students to read the final clauses back into the original sentences.

Resolution Combines Two Clauses

Resolve one complementary pair, P and \neg P:

\frac{P\lor A\qquad\neg P\lor B}{A\lor B}

The derived clause is called the resolvent. Keep every other literal.

P is the pivot atom. A and B can stand for the remaining disjunctions, including an empty remainder. Resolution is a sound consequence step, not generally an equivalence rewrite: replacing both parents by only the resolvent can lose information. In a refutation procedure, add derived clauses to the existing set.

[Sources]

  • AIMA Chapter 7, propositional resolution. Original SVG derivation diagram.

Why the Resolution Rule Is Sound

Suppose both P\lor A and \neg P\lor B are true.

Case What the parents force Therefore
P=\text{false} The first clause requires A A\lor B is true
P=\text{true} The second clause requires B A\lor B is true

Special cases:

\frac{P\qquad\neg P\lor Q}{Q} \qquad\qquad \frac{P\qquad\neg P}{\bot}

The empty clause, written \bot here, is false under every assignment.

Some texts use a square or an empty pair of braces for the empty clause. Here bottom denotes that contradictory clause; do not confuse the empty clause with an empty clause set, whose conjunction is true. The unit example on the left is modus ponens after implication elimination.

Prove Reachability by Refutation

Recall A: route A open, B: route B open, G: reachable.

\text{KB}=(A\lor B)\land(A\rightarrow G)\land(B\rightarrow G)

To prove G, add the negated query \neg G and convert to clauses:

ID Clause Origin
1 A\lor B At least one route is open
2 \neg A\lor G A\rightarrow G
3 \neg B\lor G B\rightarrow G
4 \neg G Negated query

This is the same example students classified by models earlier. We are temporarily supposing the destination is not reachable and checking whether that can coexist with the KB. Keep these clause IDs visible or write them on the board for the next slide.

A Resolution Proof Is a Checkable Trace

Start: 1: A\lor B, 2:\neg A\lor G, 3:\neg B\lor G, 4:\neg G.

New ID Parents Resolve on Derived clause
5 2 and 4 G \neg A
6 1 and 5 A B
7 3 and 6 B G
8 4 and 7 G \bot

\text{KB}\land\neg G is unsatisfiable. Therefore \text{KB}\models G.

Reveal and discuss one resolvent per click. Read parents and pivot aloud. Ask why line 8 proves the original query, not its negation. A different valid proof order is acceptable. This proof avoids exhaustive truth-table enumeration.

Do Not Cancel Two Pairs at Once

Consider these clauses:

P\lor Q,\qquad\neg P\lor\neg Q

Can we erase both complementary pairs and conclude \bot?

No. The assignment P=\text{true}, Q=\text{false} satisfies both parents.

Resolving only on P gives Q\lor\neg Q, a tautology, not a contradiction.

Give students a brief opportunity to challenge the tempting but invalid cancellation. Resolving on Q instead gives P OR NOT P, also a tautology. A sound rule cannot turn a satisfiable set into a contradictory consequence.

Unit Propagation Connects Back to Search

A unit clause forces its only literal to be true. Repeat after simplifying.

For \{A\lor B,\ \neg A\lor G,\ \neg B\lor G,\ \neg G\}:

Forced assignments can prune search. In general, no unit clauses does not mean the problem is solved; branching may still be needed.

This connects to constraint propagation from the preceding CSP lectures. A SAT solver can mix propagation with branching; this slide is not a full DPLL lecture. In this particular clause set, the unit clauses suffice to find a contradiction. Unit propagation alone is not complete for general CNF satisfiability.

Exit Exercise: What Changed for the Robot?

Again, B: battery ready, C: corridor clear, M: movement permitted.

\text{KB}_0=\{B,\ (B\land C)\rightarrow M\}

Consider two separate updates to this original KB:

  1. Add C. Does M follow?
  2. Instead, add \neg M. Does \neg C follow?

Write the rule as a clause, then justify both answers with resolution.

Allow two minutes of individual work before comparing answers. Keep the two updates separate. Students should get the clause NOT B OR NOT C OR M. This question returns to the opener but adds information, testing whether students understand why the answer can change.

The Added Fact Determines the Conclusion

The rule becomes \neg B\lor\neg C\lor M.

Resolving with known B first gives \neg C\lor M.

Update 1: Add C

\frac{\neg C\lor M\qquad C}{M}

Movement permission follows.

Update 2: Add \neg M

\frac{\neg C\lor M\qquad\neg M}{\neg C}

The corridor cannot be clear under these premises.

The original KB left M undetermined. New facts eliminate possible worlds.

Both are direct resolution derivations. To express either as a refutation proof, add the negation of its conclusion and resolve the derived unit clause with that assumption to obtain bottom. The second conclusion is logical, not a claim that permission always measures physical corridor safety; it follows because the encoded rule and B are assumed true.

Takeaways

  • Entailment: the query holds in every model of the KB.
  • Countermodel: one compatible world falsifies the query.
  • CNF: an AND of clauses; each clause is an OR of literals.
  • Resolution: remove one complementary pair and keep the rest.
  • Refutation: show the KB plus the negated query is inconsistent.

Support a conclusion with a proof. Reject entailment with a countermodel. Do not confuse either with a guess.
