Learning Through Interaction

In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent, the decision-maker, improves how it acts through interaction with an environment, the world it acts on.

A reward is a numeric feedback signal. It scores an outcome without necessarily telling the agent which action it should have taken.

An action can affect both the next reward and the situations the agent encounters later.

RL seeks an action rule that improves expected accumulated reward through experience. A reward signal is not a supervised target specifying an ideal action. The robot is an illustrative agent, not a claim about a deployed delivery system. Reused public-domain OpenClipart toy robot: https://openclipart.org/detail/325644/toy-robot. Conceptual background: the supplied Spring 2025 RL Beamer lecture and https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/rl/rl.html.

A Delivery Robot’s Choices

At depot D, the robot can finish a small job or visit pickup stop J for a larger job. At J, it can deliver or return to D.

All arrows have certain outcomes here. Rewards are invented points. Pickup and return each cost 1 point.

An episode ends when a job finishes at F. This is a terminal state: after entry, there are no further rewards or choices.

This replaces the anonymous positive-cycle example with a new constructed delivery model. Rewards belong to transitions: the +4 or +8 is received when entering F, so later value at F is zero. The actions are state-dependent: small and pickup at D, deliver and return at J. A policy that forever alternates pickup and return incurs costs, rather than collecting a positive delivery reward without delivering. We will use discount factor 0.9. The example is fictional, not measured delivery data.

Markov Decision Processes

A Markov decision process (MDP) specifies states, choices, and their consequences. Write s for the current state, a for an action, and s' for the next state.

Part Meaning Delivery example
\mathcal S Set of states \{D,J,F\}
\mathcal A(s) Actions available at s At D: small or pickup
P(s'\mid s,a) Next-state probability P(J\mid D,\text{pickup})=1
R(s,a) Expected immediate reward R(D,\text{pickup})=-1
\gamma Discount factor for later rewards We choose \gamma=0.9

The Markov property says the current state contains the information needed to predict the next state and reward, given the action. Include battery level if it affects what the robot can do.

The Markov property concerns conditional distributions, not deterministic motion or absence of randomness. Location alone may not be a sufficient state if hidden battery level changes outcomes. Our simplified three-state model assumes such details do not alter its specified consequences. R(s,a) is the expected one-step reward; a transition-dependent reward convention can also be used but is not needed here. All examples use ordinary finite discounted MDPs. Reference: https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/mdp/markov-decision-processes.html.

A Policy Is an Action Rule

A policy \pi tells the agent how to act in each state.

  • A deterministic policy chooses one action: \pi(s)=a.
  • A stochastic policy assigns probabilities: \pi(a\mid s) is the probability of choosing a in state s.

This policy chooses \pi(D)=\text{pickup} and \pi(J)=\text{deliver}. There is no decision at terminal state F.

A policy describes what to do for every possible nonterminal state, not just a prewritten route from one start. The highlighted arrows illustrate a deterministic policy in our deterministic environment. A stochastic policy and stochastic transitions are separate sources of randomness. The chosen route is an example policy; we will establish its optimality later rather than assume it now.

Return and Discounting

The return G_t adds future rewards from decision step t. The discount factor \gamma gives less weight to rewards received later:

G_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma r_{t+2}+\gamma^2r_{t+3}+\cdots, \qquad 0\leq\gamma<1.

Here r_{t+1} is the reward received after action t. After termination, all remaining rewards are zero.

At \gamma=0.9: small job gives 4; pickup then delivery gives -1+0.9\cdot8=6.2.

The immediate reward has weight 1, not gamma. The second reward has weight gamma. Gamma controls how later reward enters this objective; the points are not units of time or dollars. At gamma=0 an agent only cares about its next reward, while larger gamma gives later reward more influence. We restrict to gamma<1 for the finite discounted guarantees discussed later. Some episodic models use gamma=1 under additional assumptions, but no such assumption is needed today.

State Values and Action Values

A value predicts expected return. Let S_t be the state and A_t the chosen action at step t. The expectation \mathbb E_\pi averages outcomes while following policy \pi.

V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s].

Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a].

For the highlighted policy: V^\pi(D)=6.2. Choosing small once, then following \pi, gives Q^\pi(D,\text{small})=4.

State value follows the policy from the current state. Action value fixes the first action, then follows the policy afterward. In the example Q^pi(D,pickup)=6.2 and Q^pi(D,small)=4, while V^pi(J)=8 and V^pi(F)=0. The expectation accounts for stochastic policies and stochastic transitions in general; today’s base example has neither. We will use the shorter V(s) for a current estimate when it need not equal the exact value of any fixed policy.

A Model and Experience

A model predicts consequences through P and R. Planning uses that model to compare future choices.

  • Model-based RL learns P and R, then plans with them.
  • Model-free RL learns values or a policy without an explicit P.

With known P and R, these are planning methods. An RL agent can learn the model first.

Model-based approaches can use a provided model, a learned model, or both. Value iteration and policy iteration with a fully known model are dynamic-programming planning methods, not by themselves procedures for learning an unknown environment from samples. Model-free methods still learn representations or predictions; model-free does not mean no model object or no neural network of any kind. Avoid introducing unexplained names such as SARSA here. The later bandit and model-free lectures supply those methods in context. Reference: https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/rl/rl.html.

One-Step Lookahead and Bellman Equations

One-step lookahead scores an action using its immediate reward and a current estimate V of later value:

\operatorname{LookAhead}(s,a,V) =R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V(s').

A Bellman equation expresses value recursively. For a fixed policy,

V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s) \operatorname{LookAhead}(s,a,V^\pi).

The sums average over possible next states and policy choices. For V=V^\pi, the lookahead score equals Q^\pi(s,a).

A greedy policy chooses an action with the largest lookahead score. GreedyPolicy(V) below means this one-step comparison, including future value.

R(s,a) is an expected immediate reward, so it can be written outside the successor-state expectation. Correlation between reward and successor state does not change the expectation of their sum. State-dependent sums range only over available actions. At a terminal state we set value zero without performing a maximization over an empty action set. One-step lookahead still reasons about long-term consequences through V; it is not simply maximizing immediate reward. The fixed-policy equation averages rather than maximizing over actions. Reference: the supplied Beamer Bellman frame, revised for notation and teaching order.

Value Iteration

Value iteration repeatedly replaces each value by its best lookahead score. A sweep updates every nonterminal state once.

V = 0 for all states, including terminal F
repeat
old = copy(V)
for each nonterminal state s
V(s) = maxa LookAhead(s, a, old)
until largestChange(V, old) < threshold
return GreedyPolicy(V)

largestChange is the largest absolute value change across states. threshold is a chosen small positive stopping tolerance.

Every update in this sweep reads old. The terminal value stays zero. We call the estimates after k sweeps V_k.

This is synchronous value iteration, matching the numerical walkthrough. An in-place implementation is another valid variant but yields different intermediate sweeps. max ranges over actions available at s. With zero initial values, V_k can also be interpreted as an optimal k-step truncated-horizon value. A positive stopping threshold gives approximate values in general; it is not proof of exact convergence or a universal policy-error bound. Our small example happens to reach an exact fixed point after two sweeps. Reference: https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/mdp/value-iteration.html.

When a Move Is Uncertain

Modified scenario: pickup from D reaches J with probability 0.8, but leaves the robot at D with probability 0.2. Either outcome costs 1 point.

Using current estimates V(D)=4 and V(J)=8:

\operatorname{LookAhead}(D,\text{pickup},V) =-1+0.9(0.8\cdot8+0.2\cdot4)=5.48.

Average over outcomes using their probabilities. This is one lookahead calculation, not the modified model’s converged value.

This slide deliberately changes only the pickup transition; the deterministic base model resumes afterward. The reward remains -1 for either possible successor, so R(D,pickup)=-1. The estimates 4 and 8 are inputs to the calculation, not asserted optimal values of the modified model. We include the stay-at-D outcome rather than silently dropping failure probability. If iterated to convergence in this variant, the best D value would be 4.76/.82, about 5.804878, not 5.48; that solution is not needed here.

Policy Iteration

Policy iteration alternates evaluation, computing the current policy’s values, with improvement, choosing better actions using those values.

pi = any deterministic policy
repeat
V = EvaluatePolicy(pi)
newPi = GreedyPolicy(V, keepTies=pi)
if newPi == pi: return pi
pi = newPi

EvaluatePolicy solves the fixed-policy Bellman equations. When several actions tie for the largest score, GreedyPolicy keeps the current action.

Evaluation predicts the current policy’s long-term outcome. Improvement compares all available first actions while using those evaluated future values.

Exact evaluation can solve a linear system or iterate the fixed-policy Bellman expectation updates to convergence. It is more than one value-iteration-style sweep. Stable tie handling avoids cycling among policies with equal value. There is no decision to evaluate or improve at F. Model-free algorithms later learn from sampled experience rather than requiring an exact model-based EvaluatePolicy call. Reference: https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/mdp/policies-iteration.html.

Evaluating the Current Policy

Return to the original certain-outcome model. Start with policy \pi_0: \pi_0(D)=\text{small} and \pi_0(J)=\text{return}.

V^{\pi_0}(D)=4+0.9V^{\pi_0}(F)=4.

V^{\pi_0}(J)=-1+0.9V^{\pi_0}(D)=2.6.

These values describe following \pi_0, including its poor choice to return from the pickup stop.

The terminal value V^pi0(F)=0 is shown in the diagram and follows from the reward timing established earlier. Starting from J, this policy returns to D with reward -1 and then completes the small job for 4, giving -1+.9*4=2.6. Even if a trajectory starting at D never visits J under pi0, policy evaluation still defines what would happen when starting at J. A policy is an action rule for every state.

Improving the Policy

Use the evaluated values V^{\pi_0}(D)=4, V^{\pi_0}(J)=2.6, and V^{\pi_0}(F)=0.

State Current action’s score Alternative score New action
D small: 4 pickup: -1+0.9\cdot2.6=1.34 small
J return: -1+0.9\cdot4=2.6 deliver: 8 deliver

Only J changes at first. Reevaluate this new policy: its values are V(D)=4 and V(J)=8.

The next improvement changes D to pickup, since -1+0.9\cdot8=6.2>4.

All action scores in the table use the previous fixed policy’s values, not a partially improved successor policy. Call the first improved policy pi1: small at D and deliver at J. Evaluating pi1 gives (4,8,0). Improving again gives pi2: pickup at D and deliver at J. Evaluating pi2 gives (6.2,8,0). The following slide checks stability under this final evaluation. This explains why a policy-improvement round can change actions in stages.

The Final Policy

Both methods choose pickup at D and deliver at J.

State Chosen action’s score Other action’s score
D pickup: -1+0.9\cdot8=6.2 small: 4
J deliver: 8 return: -1+0.9\cdot6.2=4.58

An optimal policy \pi^* maximizes expected return from every state. Its values are V^*. No action changes here.

The final values satisfy the Bellman optimality fixed point with V(F)=0. The pickup-return cycle costs a point on every transition and cannot outperform delivery. Contrast this with the supplied example, where a positive-reward cycle made continuing more attractive than the nominal terminal rewards. We use the new model consistently rather than carrying over that example’s numbers. This optimum concerns the stated reward objective, not an independently verified real-world task.

Value Iteration and Policy Iteration

Method Main operation Practical tradeoff
Value iteration Repeated best-action value updates Cheap sweeps, potentially many of them
Policy iteration Evaluate a policy, then improve actions More work per round, often fewer rounds

For finite states and actions, bounded rewards, and 0\leq\gamma<1:

  • Value-iteration values approach V^*.
  • Exact policy iteration, keeping the old action on ties, reaches an optimal policy after finitely many changes.
  • At least one deterministic optimal policy exists in this setting.

Both require a model of consequences. Learning that model, or learning decisions directly from samples, is the next part of RL.

These guarantees refer to finite discounted MDPs with the usual unconstrained expected-return objective. Do not extend the deterministic-policy statement to every finite-horizon, constrained, or undiscounted formulation without the needed qualifications. A finite value-change stopping threshold in value iteration generally gives approximate values. There is no universal runtime winner: policy evaluation can be expensive and implementations may use approximate evaluation. References: the supplied Beamer comparison and the Berkeley value/policy iteration notes.

Quick Check

  1. If battery level affects which actions succeed, is location alone a sufficient Markov state?
  2. Why is V(F)=0 even though finishing gives a positive reward?
  3. Why does our value-iteration sweep make a copy called old?

1. No. Include battery level, or another sufficient summary, to predict consequences from the current state and action.

2. The reward arrives on entry. From the finished state onward there are no more rewards. Counting it again would double-count it.

3. To use one consistent preceding estimate. This is the synchronous update shown here. In-place updates are a different valid variant with different intermediate values.

Takeaways

  • RL improves action choices through interaction and reward.
  • An MDP specifies states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and discounting.
  • A policy chooses actions. Its value predicts expected return.
  • Bellman equations connect current value to successor-state values.
  • Value iteration updates estimates using the best lookahead score.
  • Policy iteration evaluates a policy, then improves its choices.

Separate immediate reward, long-term return, and the policy that determines future actions.
