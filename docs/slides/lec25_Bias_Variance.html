Predicting Campus Delivery Times

A campus courier team wants to predict a route’s duration before departure.

  • Inputs: planned distance z (km) and stops c.
  • Output: recorded route duration y (minutes).
  • One observation: one completed route.

We have 50 training routes. Here are three one-stop routes from the records:

Route Distance z (km) Stops c Duration y (min)
A 1 1 11
B 2 1 11

These are invented teaching records, not measured campus data.

This running example is a realistic prediction task with deliberately constructed numbers, not a claim about an actual courier service. Only inputs known before departure are eligible features. Actual travel duration, eventual waiting time, and completion timestamps would not be valid inputs for predicting the same route before it starts. The three displayed routes are a small excerpt; the full illustrative training set contains 50 routes with varying distances and stop counts. No complete 50-route dataset or real fitted model is claimed. Later we explicitly assume a simple data-generating rule to make the otherwise unknown meaning of s visible.

Fitting Data and Generalizing

Generalization means predicting well on new routes. Observations may include noise: variation not predictable from the recorded inputs, such as random waiting delays.

Data Role
Training Fit coefficients using our 50 completed routes
Validation Compare model choices on separate routes, without refitting
Test Check the final choice on further routes used for neither fitting nor choosing

If a model remembers every training route’s duration, must it predict the next route accurately?

No. Matching the training observations can include matching their noise.

The supplied Spring 2025 lecture motivates generalization through linear regression. The examples here assume fresh examples come from the same distribution as training data. A representative independent test set estimates generalization performance, but is not a guarantee about every future input. The notebook’s comparisons across many model sizes are an experiment, not a procedure for selecting a deployed model using its test set.

Linear Regression and Its Parameters

A parameter is a number fitted from data. For one-stop routes, try predicting duration from distance alone:

\hat y=b+wz.

  • b: intercept, predicted minutes at z=0.
  • w: slope, extra minutes per kilometer.
  • \hat y: predicted duration; y: recorded duration.

The dots are observations. The line is one candidate model.

For b=8, w=2, and a 3 km route: \hat y=8+2(3)=14 minutes.

The displayed line is a candidate, not the least-squares optimum for these three observations. Its intercept is the model’s prediction at z=0, an extrapolated baseline, not a claim that zero-distance deliveries necessarily occur. The later constructed full rule is 5+2z+3c. For the one-stop subset c=1, its mean is 8+2z, so this candidate agrees with the assumed mean but need not minimize the observed noisy sample loss. In neural-network terminology an intercept is sometimes called a bias parameter; that is different from the statistical bias discussed later. The letter z is route distance; later x denotes a vector of features.

Residuals and Mean Squared Error

A residual is observed minus predicted output: y_i-\hat y_i. Here i identifies an observation, and n is the number of observations.

Mean squared error (MSE) averages the squared residuals:

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2.

Distance (km) Observed (min) Predicted (min) Squared residual
1 11 10 1
2 11 12 1
3 16 14 4

\text{MSE}=(1+1+4)/3=2\ \text{min}^2. Minimizing this loss is least squares.

These predictions use b=8, w=2 from the previous slide. The residuals are 1,-1,2 minutes, with squared residuals 1,1,4. These are the three displayed training examples, not a complete 50-route error estimate. Squaring prevents positive and negative residuals from canceling and penalizes large errors more strongly. MSE has squared-output units. Minimizing mean squared error and minimizing the sum of squared residuals have the same minimizers when n is fixed.

Features and the Design Matrix

Features are input numbers. For the distance-only model, use \mathbf x=(1,z)^T and coefficients \boldsymbol\beta=(b,w)^T. There are p=2 features, including the constant 1.

Transpose (T) swaps rows and columns.

\hat y=\mathbf x^T\boldsymbol\beta.

A design matrix stores all inputs. We use features as rows and observations as columns:

X=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol\beta=\begin{bmatrix}8\\2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \hat{\mathbf y}=X^T\boldsymbol\beta=\begin{bmatrix}10\\12\\14\end{bmatrix}.

This excerpt has n=3 routes. The full training set has n=50. In general, X has shape p\times n.

Each column is one route. Each row is one feature used for every route.

For the example x=(1,z), beta=(b,w), and the three distances are z=1,2,3. The output vector has n entries. The n=3 matrix is only the displayed excerpt, not the full n=50 experiment. Many other libraries store examples in rows, the opposite convention. In the notebook, the candidate features are independent Gaussian inputs and there is no added constant feature. A constant counts as a feature when one is included, as in this illustration. Linear regression is linear in the coefficients; constructed features such as z^2 can represent a curved relationship.

Fitting with a Pseudoinverse

Optimization chooses parameters to minimize a loss. Here we minimize training MSE.

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is a matrix operation that provides a least-squares solution even when an ordinary inverse is unavailable:

\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X^T)^\dagger\mathbf y.

  • Superscript \dagger denotes the pseudoinverse.
  • \mathbf y holds the n observed training outputs.
  • If several fits have the same minimum loss, this chooses the minimum-norm one: the coefficient vector with the smallest Euclidean length.

For the three displayed routes, least squares gives \hat b=23/3\approx7.67 and \hat w=2.5. Their training MSE falls from 2 to 25/18\approx1.39\ \text{min}^2.

For the three displayed routes only, the design has rows (1,1,1) and (1,2,3), with observed outputs (11,11,16). The pseudoinverse gives beta=(23/3,5/2), predictions (61/6,76/6,91/6), and residuals (5/6,-10/6,5/6). Its training MSE is 25/18, below the chosen candidate’s MSE 2. Fitting the observed sample better does not establish better prediction on fresh routes. These values are not claimed estimates from all 50 routes.

The Euclidean length of beta is sqrt(sum_j beta_j^2). For an underdetermined constraint beta_1+beta_2=2, (1,1) has smaller length than (2,0); the minimum-norm solution is (1,1). NumPy’s pinv uses singular value decomposition with a numerical cutoff for very small singular values. Reference: https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.linalg.pinv.html.

With this feature-row convention, the ordinary unique least-squares formula is (X XT)(-1) X y when X has full row rank p, so p cannot exceed n. Merely having more observations than features does not guarantee this independence condition. The pseudoinverse handles both sides of the interpolation threshold. Gradient descent is a different optimization method that repeatedly adjusts parameters using loss derivatives; stochastic gradient descent uses subsets of examples for its updates. Neither algorithm is required by this notebook.

Model Size: Delivery Inputs

Model complexity means flexibility. Each selected feature gets one fitted coefficient, counted in p, including the intercept.

Size Features Candidate prediction
p=1 Constant 1 \hat y=b
p=2 1,z \hat y=b+w_1z
p=3 1,z,c \hat y=b+w_1z+w_2c

From the same observation, we can construct several features:

\mathbf x=(1,z,z^2,c,c^2,c^3)^T,\qquad p=6.

These powers allow curves while the model remains linear in its coefficients.

For z=2,c=2, the features are (1,2,4,2,4,8)^T. This is still one observation. Our 50 routes still give n=50.

These are candidate models with different feature sets, not claimed estimates from a real dataset. In the three-feature model, x_1=1, x_2=z, and x_3=c; p counts the intercept as well as slopes. For b=5, w_1=2, w_2=3, a 2 km, two-stop route has predicted duration 5+2(2)+3(2)=15 minutes. The one-stop version of 5+2z+3c is 8+2z, matching the preceding illustration.

Constructed features are numerical functions of the same recorded inputs, not extra observations. The six-feature predictor is b+w_1z+w_2z2+w_3c+w_4c2+w_5*c^3. Its dependence on the fitted coefficients is linear even though it can curve with distance and stop count. For z=2,c=2, its feature vector is (1,2,4,2,4,8). The equal values in this one vector do not mean these features agree across all possible routes. With our feature-row matrix convention, adding three constructed features changes the full matrix from 3 by 50 to 6 by 50. It does not create more training information or guarantee independent feature rows. Under the later assumed true rule 5+2z+3c, the extra power terms have zero true coefficients; increasing p does not automatically increase s. Adding flexibility can reduce bias but also increase sensitivity to training data.

Delivery Data: n, p, and s

To make the unknown true rule visible, suppose our teaching data follow:

y=\underbrace{5+2z+3c}_{\text{mean duration in minutes}}+\varepsilon.

Here \varepsilon is unpredictable variation around the mean, such as random waiting. It has mean zero and standard deviation 2 minutes.

Symbol Meaning in this example
n=50 Fifty completed routes used for fitting
s=3 Three nonzero true terms: constant 1, distance z, and stops c
p Terms we choose to fit: 2 for 1,z or 3 for 1,z,c

We choose p. The true rule determines s, which we usually do not know in real data. Both counts include the constant here.

All numerical values and the true rule are explicit teaching assumptions. A feature is relevant here if its coefficient in this specified conditional mean is nonzero. We count the constant as a feature, so s=3 includes the baseline; the number of varying relevant inputs is two. The extra job tags are five numeric random values assigned independently of distance, stops, duration noise, and other routes before departure, with true coefficients zero. Their apparent association with observed delays in a finite training sample is accidental. The full distribution of waiting noise is not important for the definitions; assume independent zero-mean noise with standard deviation 2 minutes. Negative noise means a faster-than-average trip, not a negative duration. Actual delivery data need not follow this simple rule, and a realistic study would investigate the feature set and data distribution rather than claiming s is known.

Underfitting and Overfitting

Underfitting: the model is too rigid to capture the broad pattern.

Overfitting: matching training-specific fluctuations harms prediction on new examples.

For this separate curved-pattern illustration, polynomial degree is the largest power of the input. Degree 2 is quadratic. These models are linear in their coefficients.

Dots are the same seven observations in each panel. The dashed gray curve is the underlying pattern.

A perfect fit to the dots can be a poor fit to the pattern between them.

This constructed example uses the noise-free rule y=z^2 and observed points z=-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3 with y=9,5,0,1,0,5,9. The fitted degree-one model is 29/7 with zero slope. The fitted quadratic is 1/21+(43/42)z^2. The interpolating polynomial is 1-(181/90)z2+(79/72)z4-(31/360)z^6. All are linear least-squares models in their chosen polynomial features. Their training MSEs are about 13.265, 0.687, and zero. Degree alone is not a universal diagnosis of underfitting or overfitting.

  • Overfitting risk: five extra random numeric job tags give p=8. They may fit accidental training delays, but cannot predict the next route’s delay.

Useful inputs capture a repeatable pattern. More inputs also create opportunities to fit coincidences.

A distance-only model predicts the same value for equal distances regardless of its fitted intercept and slope. It therefore cannot represent a true mean that differs with stop count at a fixed distance. The 8+2z candidate happens to be correct for one-stop routes and is six minutes too low for three-stop routes at the same distance. The three-feature candidate uses the assumed true coefficients here for an easily checked comparison; finite-data estimates would generally differ. Random job tags are independent numerical inputs, not an encoded target or a unique identifier seen only after completion. They have no true predictive information by construction but may correlate with noise in a finite sample. Extra inputs create a risk of overfitting, not a guarantee that every larger model performs worse.

Bias and Variance Across Training Sets

Fix a 2 km, two-stop route, whose mean duration is 15 minutes. Imagine refitting on fresh sets of 50 training routes.

  • Bias: average prediction minus the true noise-free mean output.
  • Variance: how much the prediction changes across those training datasets.

A is about 5 minutes too low on average. B changes more across training sets. The route stays the same; the training records change.

The five displayed predictions per method are constructed values, not estimates from actual courier experiments or the notebook. Treating these five equally likely outcomes as an illustrative distribution gives mean 10 minutes, bias -5 minutes and variance .5 squared minutes for A, versus mean 15 minutes, bias 0 and variance 18 squared minutes for B. Each point is the prediction from a different training dataset for the identical fixed route, not a different route or the same model tested on five days. Bias and variance are properties of a learning procedure at an input under repeated training samples. Reference: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/ensemble/plot_bias_variance.html.

Prediction Error and Irreducible Noise

Irreducible noise is output variation that the available features cannot predict, even if we know the true mean rule.

Here expected means averaged over fresh training datasets and fresh independent output noise, at a fixed input:

\begin{aligned} \text{Expected squared error} &=\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}\\ &\quad+\text{noise variance}. \end{aligned}

The noise standard deviation \sigma is the square root of its variance. In our delivery example, \sigma=2 minutes, so \sigma^2=4\ \text{min}^2.

Even a perfect 15-minute mean prediction cannot foresee this route’s random waiting delay.

Assume observed output Y=f(x)+epsilon with independent zero-mean noise epsilon. Bias is E_D[f_hat_D(x)]-f(x), variance is E_D[(f_hat_D(x)-E_D[f_hat_D(x)])^2], and noise variance is E[epsilon^2]. The identity concerns squared prediction error, not arbitrary losses, and follows by expanding the square. Averaging further over test inputs gives an integrated version. In the delivery illustration, even knowledge of the true mean rule leaves expected squared error 4 min^2 on a fresh noisy route. The separate notebook uses noise standard deviation .2 in its own output units, so its corresponding noise variance is .04. The bias-variance identity remains valid when a model displays double descent.

Model Complexity and Validation

Compare delivery models on the same training routes. Horizontal: increasing p. Vertical: MSE. The curves are schematic, not measured delivery results.

  • Training error: MSE on the examples used to fit the coefficients.
  • Validation error: MSE on held-out examples; no refitting on them.

Choose p using the lowest validation MSE. Keep the test set for the final check.

For nested feature sets, fixed training examples, and unregularized least squares solved to the global optimum, the minimum achievable training MSE cannot increase when features are added: setting the new coefficients to zero preserves the old solution. Unseen error has no corresponding monotonicity guarantee. The curves illustrate a possible validation pattern and are not fitted to the notebook results. Reducing complexity, collecting more informative data, and regularization can help; regularization means adding a penalty or constraint to the fitting procedure, such as discouraging large coefficients. A held-out validation estimate itself has sampling uncertainty.

A Separate Controlled Experiment

Each observation now has 250 independent random inputs, x_1,\ldots,x_{250}. Only the first ten affect the output. We include no constant feature:

y=a_1x_1+\cdots+a_{10}x_{10}+\varepsilon.

The a_1,\ldots,a_{10} are fixed, nonzero true coefficients. Independent, zero-mean noise \varepsilon has standard deviation 0.2 in this experiment’s output units.

Symbol Meaning Value or choice
n Number of training observations 50

p Number of features we let the fitted model use 1 to 250

The delivery rule had s=3, counting the constant. This separate experiment has s=10 and no constant. The meaning of s is unchanged.

This is a new constructed example with measured random features, rather than polynomial features of one z. In the experiment and its later notebook demonstration, all input coordinates are independent standard normal variables. The ten true coefficients have Euclidean length 1. The remaining 240 true coefficients are zero. Independent zero-mean Gaussian output noise has standard deviation .2. The data-generating coefficients stay fixed across training and test datasets, and no constant feature is added. The noise-free mean output is the sum of the first ten contributions. The word true refers to this constructed rule, not to what a fitted model discovers. Index j identifies a feature. In a real dataset we usually do not know the true rule or which features matter.

Fitted Features and True Features

Recall: n=50 training observations, s=10 truly relevant features. We fit one coefficient for each of the first p inputs:

\hat y=\hat\beta_1x_1+\cdots+\hat\beta_px_p.

The \hat\beta coefficients are estimates fitted from training data.

Choice of p Comparison What the model can use
5 p<s Only x_1,\ldots,x_5; misses five relevant features
20 s\leq p<n All ten relevant features, plus ten irrelevant ones
80 p>n All ten relevant features, plus seventy irrelevant ones

Underparameterized means p<n. Overparameterized means p>n.

s describes the true rule. p describes the model we choose.

The hat on beta indicates coefficients estimated from the training data. In this controlled experiment, the first s features are precisely the relevant ones. Thus p>=s includes all relevant features only because of this specified ordering; an arbitrary selection of p>=s inputs need not do so. Irrelevant means a zero coefficient in the true conditional mean, not necessarily a fitted coefficient of zero. The least-squares fit can assign nonzero coefficients to these inputs by matching chance associations with training noise. Underparameterized is not a synonym for underfitting, and overparameterized is not a synonym for harmful overfitting. The p=5 fit cannot recover the omitted independent inputs from the included ones.

Interpolation: Zero Training Error

Interpolation means matching every observed training output exactly, including its noise:

\hat y_i=y_i\quad\text{for every training observation }i.

Each of the n observations gives one equation to satisfy. We have p fitted coefficients available.

  • With p<n, we generally cannot satisfy all n noisy equations exactly.
  • In our independent random-feature example, exact fitting becomes possible at p=n=50: the interpolation threshold.
  • With p>n, many exact fits exist. The pseudoinverse selects the minimum-norm one.

The earlier degree-6 polynomial also interpolates: its seven coefficients fit all seven dots. Zero training MSE says nothing by itself about new dots.

The selected training design has shape n by p after transposing our feature-row matrix. Independent Gaussian entries give rank min(n,p) with probability one. Thus at p=n it is invertible almost surely and can match any response vector; for p>n it has full row rank and has infinitely many solutions. For p<n, the n noisy constraints are generally inconsistent. The parameter-count argument requires independent constraints and a sufficiently expressive design: repeating one feature many times would not produce this threshold. The earlier seven distinct input locations give an invertible degree-six Vandermonde design. Exact arithmetic is assumed in this explanation; numerical training MSE may be near rather than identically zero.

Double Descent

Double descent: unseen error can fall, rise near interpolation, then fall again for larger models.

Near p=n, small changes in noisy training outputs can cause large coefficient changes and unstable new predictions.

Benign overfitting describes settings where fitting even the training noise still permits accurate prediction on new data.

p>n alone guarantees neither double descent nor benign overfitting.

The figure is deliberately schematic. Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal, Reconciling modern machine learning practice and the bias-variance trade-off: https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11118. Bartlett, Long, Lugosi, and Tsigler, Benign Overfitting in Linear Regression: https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.11300. Benign overfitting can yield near-optimal prediction under particular distributional conditions; exact training fit alone is insufficient evidence. The phenomenon occurs in some linear models as well as more complex learners.

The notebook’s isotropic Gaussian, fixed-signal experiment need not improve indefinitely after interpolation. Its overparameterized expected-error formula has a growing signal-bias contribution and a decreasing noise-amplification contribution. Consequently the large-p branch can turn upward again. The schematic is not a promise that arbitrary further enlargement improves that experiment.

Takeaways

  • n counts training cases; p fitted terms; s nonzero true terms.
  • Bias describes systematic offset. Variance describes sensitivity to the training dataset.
  • Unpredictable output noise remains even with a correct mean model.
  • Parameter count, interpolation, and harmful overfitting are different concepts.
  • Evaluate on fresh data. A perfect training fit is not a certificate of generalization.

For delivery models, the final check is accurate timing on new routes, beyond the records used to fit or choose the model.
