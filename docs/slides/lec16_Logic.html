What Does the Rule Actually Permit?

A lab’s access rule says:

If a person has a valid badge, the door unlocks.

Does this also say that the door stays locked for everyone without a valid badge?

No. A rule can promise one outcome without ruling out other ways to reach it.

50-minute teaching plan, including discussion: slides 1-6, 8 minutes; 7-12, 9 minutes; 13-19, 12 minutes; 20-26, 10 minutes; 27-31, 11 minutes. Ask students to commit to an answer before revealing it. Main activities: translation on slide 11, formula evaluation on slide 20, and policy debugging on slides 27-29. This lecture develops representation and truth conditions. The following lecture develops entailment, CNF, and proof procedures.

The badge rule is a material implication about one fixed person and one evaluation of the access decision. It is not a description of an electrical actuator or a complete security policy.

Logic Separates Meaning from Wording

Logic provides a precise language for representing facts and constraints.

  • Representation: What exactly does the rule assert?
  • Evaluation: Does a particular situation satisfy the rule?
  • Reasoning: What else follows from the facts and rules?

Connect to CSPs: a logical formula also restricts combinations of values. A precise formula can still encode the wrong English meaning. Reasoning does not repair a mistranslated requirement. The diagram is original course artwork.

Which Sentences Are Propositions?

A proposition is a statement with a truth value, once its context is fixed.

Sentence A proposition?
“The lab door is unlocked now.” Yes, even if we have not checked
“Two plus three equals six.” Yes; it is false
“Unlock the lab door!” No; this is a command
“Is the lab door unlocked?” No; this is a question
“x is greater than three.” Not a closed proposition until x is specified

Having a truth value is different from knowing that truth value.

Ask whether an unobserved fact counts. Classical propositional logic uses two truth values, not a third value for our ignorance. Fix the person, door, and time whenever natural language would otherwise leave them implicit. The open expression involving x motivates predicates later; do not introduce quantifiers yet.

Give Each Atom a Stable Meaning

For one person, at one access decision:

Symbol Atomic proposition
B The person has a valid badge
V The person is an approved visitor
E An emergency is active
D The door unlocks
  • An atom is a proposition that we treat as indivisible.
  • Symbols are names, not numerical measurements.
  • Keep the same meaning everywhere in a formula.

These four atoms will return in the final policy activity. They are a deliberately simplified access decision, not advice for physical security systems. Ask why E should not mean “no emergency” on one slide and “emergency” on another.

Syntax Groups; Semantics Evaluates

Syntax: how a formula is built. Semantics: when it is true.

(B \lor V) \land \neg E

Parentheses matter: (B \lor V) \land \neg E is not B \lor (V \land \neg E).

Read the tree bottom-up. The top-level connective combines two subformulas. The two displayed formulas differ when B is true and E is true. Prefer explicit parentheses over depending on a convention about precedence. Original diagram.

A and B mean that the respective route is selected, not that it is open. Ask students which factor rules out neither route, and which factor rules out both routes. The English word “or” can be ambiguous; ask what the task requires.

Build a Requirement in Pieces

“A valid badge or approved visitor, and no active emergency.”

  1. Either authorization method: B \lor V.
  1. No emergency: \neg E.
  1. Both requirements:

(B \lor V) \land \neg E

Translate the parts, then connect them. Negation applies only to its stated scope.

Your Turn: Translate the Requirement

Let B mean “battery ready” and C mean “corridor clear.”

  1. The battery is ready and the corridor is clear.
  2. It is not the case that both conditions hold.
  3. Neither condition holds.

Write one formula for each. Are the last two equivalent?

Give pairs two minutes, then invite different formulas. This is a new small scenario: explicitly redefine B as battery ready for slides 11-12. The following slides restore the badge vocabulary. Do not reveal the next slide prematurely. A common mistake is translating “not both” as “neither.”

Not Both Is Not the Same as Neither

Requirement Formula
Both conditions hold B \land C
Not both hold \neg(B \land C)
Neither holds \neg B \land \neg C

Take B=\text{true} and C=\text{false}:

  • Not both is true.
  • Neither is false.

One situation where the outputs differ is enough to show that two formulas are not equivalent.

Implication Rules Out One Case

Back to the lab: B = valid badge; D = door unlocks.

B \rightarrow D

B D B \rightarrow D Rule satisfied?
T T T Yes
T F F No: badge, but no unlock
F T T Yes
F F T Yes

The implication is false only when its premise is true and its conclusion is false.

A False Premise Leaves the Rule Unbroken

“If there is a valid badge, the door unlocks”: B \rightarrow D.

  • No valid badge, door locked: the rule is not violated.
  • No valid badge, door unlocked: the rule is still not violated.

This is material implication. It constrains truth values; it does not by itself express causation, timing, or a complete policy.

Which extra condition would rule out unlocking without a badge?

D \rightarrow B: unlocking requires a valid badge.

The implication is often called vacuously true when its premise is false. Use “not violated” to explain the convention, not to add a third truth value. The badge rule alone is compatible with a visitor, an override, or a faulty system unlocking the door. Do not infer a causal mechanism from a logical arrow.

“If” and “Only If” Reverse the Direction

English Formula What it requires
D if B B \rightarrow D Badge is sufficient for unlocking
D only if B D \rightarrow B Badge is necessary for unlocking
  • Sufficient: enough to guarantee the conclusion.
  • Necessary: required whenever the conclusion holds.

“The robot moves only if its battery is ready.” Let M = moves, R = ready. Which arrow?

M \rightarrow R. Read it as “movement requires readiness.”

Reversing the Arrow Changes the Rule

Name Formula Equivalent to the original?
Original B \rightarrow D Yes
Converse D \rightarrow B No
Inverse \neg B \rightarrow \neg D No
Contrapositive \neg D \rightarrow \neg B Yes

Try B=\text{false}, D=\text{true}. Which rows are true?

Original and contrapositive: true. Converse and inverse: false.

The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other, but not to the original. Here “not equivalent” means that there exists an assignment where the values differ, not that they differ on every assignment. Naming is less important than checking the truth conditions.

Unless Needs a Careful Translation

“The robot returns to its dock unless an emergency is active.”

Let R = returns and E = emergency active.

\neg E \rightarrow R \qquad\equiv\qquad E \lor R

  • No emergency: returning is required.
  • Emergency active: this rule does not determine whether it returns.

To also prohibit returning during an emergency, add E \rightarrow \neg R. That is an extra requirement.

Use the conventional inclusive reading of unless. Everyday usage can imply more; clarify the intended behavior before formalizing it. Returning during an emergency is neither required nor forbidden by the first rule alone. This is not automatically an XOR.

Evaluate from the Inside Out

For the access condition:

F=(B \lor V) \land \neg E

Evaluate F for B=\text{false}, V=\text{true}, E=\text{false}.

  1. B \lor V = \text{true}.
  1. \neg E = \text{true}.
  1. F = \text{true} \land \text{true} = \text{true}.

Give students 30 seconds to evaluate before stepping through the fragments. Then ask what changes when E becomes true. Explain that F names the whole formula, while the tables use the letter F as an abbreviation for false; these are different contexts.

Equivalence Preserves All Truth Values

A \equiv B

means that formulas A and B agree under every assignment to their atoms.

Goal What is enough?
Prove equivalence with a truth table All output rows agree
Disprove equivalence One row where the outputs differ
Rewrite safely Apply an established equivalence law

Equivalent formulas can replace each other inside a larger formula without changing its meaning.

A and B here are arbitrary formulas, not the badge atom from the worked scenario. Mention that equivalence is a relation between formulas. The biconditional is a connective whose truth is evaluated within a world. A and B are equivalent exactly when their biconditional is true in every world. No formal entailment notation yet.

De Morgan Moves NOT Across a Group

\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q

Not both requirements succeed: at least one fails.

\neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q

Neither option succeeds: both fail.

Negate each part and swap AND with OR. Keep the parentheses until the scope is clear.

Return to the “not both” versus “neither” exercise. Use a two-input sensor as an intuitive check: not(all ready) means any not-ready; not(any ready) means all not-ready. These equivalences are not an instruction to replace AND with OR without also negating the inputs.

Have students compute the second row aloud. Even two non-equivalent formulas may match on the first row. Four rows exhaust the two-variable assignments, so agreement here establishes equivalence. The table omits intermediate columns for space; students should add them on paper if they need them.

Simplify Without Changing the Requirement

“Valid badge and lab open, or valid badge and lab not open.”

Let B = valid badge and L = lab open.

(B \land L) \lor (B \land \neg L)

Distribute the common factor:

\equiv B \land (L \lor \neg L)

One of L and \neg L always holds:

\equiv B \land \text{true} \equiv B

The lab-open condition has canceled from the policy. Ask whether that was intended. The identity used is (X and Y) or (X and Z) equivalent to X and (Y or Z). The law of excluded middle makes L or not L true under every classical assignment. Rewriting exposes redundancies, but does not decide whether a requirement is appropriate.

A Small Toolbox for Rewriting

Law Equivalence
Double negation \neg\neg P \equiv P
Identity P \land \text{true} \equiv P; P \lor \text{false} \equiv P
Domination P \lor \text{true} \equiv \text{true}; P \land \text{false} \equiv \text{false}
Idempotence P \land P \equiv P; P \lor P \equiv P
Implication (P \rightarrow Q) \equiv \neg P \lor Q

Rewrite \neg(P \rightarrow Q) without an implication.

\neg(\neg P \lor Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q: precisely the case that violates the implication.

Mini-Lab: Does This Policy Say Enough?

B = valid badge; V = approved visitor; E = emergency; D = unlock.

The intended policy: unlock exactly when authorized and no emergency is active.

The proposed rules are:

((B \lor V) \land \neg E) \rightarrow D D \rightarrow \neg E

Could both rules be true while an unauthorized person gets in? Find an assignment or explain why not.

Allow three minutes in pairs. Suggested prompts: what happens if both B and V are false? What does the second rule actually require? Do not assume that a proposition is false just because no rule requires it to be true. The rules constrain possible access decisions; they are not a sequential program with an implicit else branch.

A Missing Direction Leaves a Gap

Try an unauthorized person, no emergency, and an unlocked door:

B=\text{false},\quad V=\text{false},\quad E=\text{false},\quad D=\text{true}

Proposed rule Evaluation
((B \lor V) \land \neg E) \rightarrow D \text{false} \rightarrow \text{true}: true
D \rightarrow \neg E \text{true} \rightarrow \text{true}: true

Missing requirement: unlocking must require authorization.

D \rightarrow (B \lor V)

All written rules can be satisfied while the intended policy is violated.

Repair the Policy with Both Directions

D \leftrightarrow ((B \lor V) \land \neg E)

  • Authorization and no emergency guarantee unlocking.
  • Unlocking requires authorization and no emergency.

What must D be in each case?

  1. Valid badge, emergency active.
  2. Approved visitor, no badge, no emergency.
  3. No badge, no visitor approval, no emergency.

False; true; false. The two directions now specify the complete Boolean decision.

Give students one minute, then reveal. The repaired biconditional is equivalent to all three implications from the preceding slides. It is complete only for this simplified Boolean authorization decision; real systems require additional details such as identity, timing, access rights, failure handling, and safety requirements. The activity demonstrates specification debugging, not deployment-ready access control.

What Propositional Logic Leaves Out

Need What changes?
Facts about many people First-order logic adds objects and predicates
“Every approved visitor has a host” Quantifiers express general rules
“The door eventually locks” Temporal logic expresses time relationships
A sensor is only 90% reliable A probabilistic model represents uncertainty

Choose the representation for the task. Today, each atom is a fixed true-or-false statement.

This replaces the old broad types-of-logic survey with a motivation for richer representations. Explain the distinction between truth values and an agent’s uncertain beliefs. No quantifier exercises here; First-Order Logic comes after Propositional Inference. Probability is a separate later part of the course.

Takeaways

  • Atoms: define one stable statement per symbol.
  • Connectives: distinguish OR, XOR, implication, and iff.
  • Translation: check “only if,” “unless,” and negation scope.
  • Truth tables: evaluate every assignment systematically.
  • Equivalence: preserve truth values when rewriting.

A formula says exactly what its truth conditions allow, not everything its author intended.
