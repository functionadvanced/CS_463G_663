Recommending a Python Help Resource

A multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem repeatedly chooses among options with unknown rewards. Each option is an arm, and choosing it is a pull.

For each request, recommend one resource. Reward is 1 if the student rates it useful, and 0 otherwise.

The highest rating average so far may belong to the best resource, or simply to the one that got lucky early.

All resource descriptions, reward sequences, means, and allocations in this lecture are constructed teaching examples, not an experiment with students. We use one basic stationary three-arm recommendation model throughout the main calculations. This simplification does not model how a recommendation changes a student’s future knowledge or which students need different resources. Those limitations return near the end.

Why the Name “Bandit”?

A lever-operated slot machine is nicknamed a one-armed bandit: its lever is the arm, and its uncertain payouts can take the player’s money.

The mathematical problem offers several unknown reward options.

In our example:

  • Arm: a resource format.
  • Pull: recommend that format.
  • Reward: its usefulness rating.

The name explanation comes from the supplied original Beamer lecture. The unchanged MAB.png cartoon was already in that project; its Beamer frame explicitly credits ChatGPT as its creator. Its original generation prompt is unavailable. It illustrates the nickname, not a literal model of the recommendation task.

Feedback and the Basic Assumptions

At round t, choose arm A_t and observe reward R_t only for that arm.

  • Stochastic means the reward is random. Our 0-or-1 reward is a Bernoulli reward.
  • Stationary means each arm’s reward distribution stays fixed. Assume independent requests from the same population and immediate feedback.

The horizon T is the number of rounds. The goal is high expected total reward over those T choices.

In the basic bandit model there is no changing external state whose transition depends on the action. The learner’s information still changes after each observation, so actions affect what it knows for future decisions. Conditional on a selected arm, rewards here are independent samples from that arm’s fixed Bernoulli distribution. We cannot observe which rating an unchosen resource would have received. This is an assumed simplified setting, not a claim that all real recommendation feedback is stationary or immediate.

Counts, Estimates, and Unknown Means

Before round t, N_t(a) counts earlier pulls of arm a. Its sample mean Q_t(a) averages the rewards observed for that arm:

Q_t(a)=\frac{\text{total observed reward from }a}{N_t(a)},\qquad N_t(a)>0.

The true mean \mu_a is the arm’s unknown average reward, here its probability of a useful rating. An observed Q_t(a) can differ from \mu_a.

The ten displayed observations form one hypothetical record before round t=11: counts sum to t-1. Every subsequent numerical choice/update uses this record or its explicitly updated round-12 version. The per-arm rows need not specify a full interleaving of all ten choices; the displayed counts and reward totals are sufficient for the rules we study. Q is an estimate of a one-round reward mean here, rather than the full state/action return of Lecture 30. Do not confuse an empirical fraction with a known success probability.

Exploitation and Exploration

Exploitation uses current estimates to choose a high-reward arm. A greedy rule always chooses an arm with the largest Q_t(a), breaking ties randomly.

Arm before round 11 Pulls Estimated mean
A: guide 6 0.500
B: worked example 3 0.333
C: video 1 0

Greedy chooses A. But the single failure for C leaves little evidence about its true mean.

Exploration tries options to gain information. It may cost reward now while helping later choices. A zero observation does not establish a zero reward probability.

We do not claim that greedy must choose A forever under this particular ten-observation history; its means can change and B has a positive estimate. The point is that greedy action selection has no deliberate mechanism for gathering evidence about ignored C. Exploration and exploitation describe purposes/selection rules, not disjoint sets of arm identities: exploration may also choose the currently greedy arm.

Epsilon-Greedy

Epsilon-greedy uses a random-choice branch with probability \epsilon, where 0\leq\epsilon\leq1. Otherwise it exploits the current estimates Q.

function EpsilonGreedy(Q, epsilon)
with probability epsilon
return UniformChoice(allArms) // explore
otherwise
return BestArm(Q) // exploit

UniformChoice makes every arm equally likely, including the current best. BestArm chooses the largest estimate and breaks ties uniformly at random.

A larger \epsilon gathers more random experience, but also spends more choices on options with lower current estimates.

The input Q is the current table of Q_t(a) values. Fixed positive epsilon keeps random exploration active; it does not make all choices eventually optimal. We do not assert a universal best epsilon or automatically transfer a convergence claim from an infinite-horizon setting to this finite exercise. Uniform exploration includes all three arms, not only the non-greedy ones. Source: the supplied Beamer epsilon-greedy material, revised to make probabilities and tie handling explicit.

Actual Selection Probabilities

Return to the record where A is the unique highest estimate. With three arms and \epsilon=0.15, each arm gets 0.15/3=0.05 probability from exploration.

P(A)=1-\epsilon+\epsilon/3=0.90,\qquad P(B)=P(C)=0.05.

Why is selecting A more likely than 1-\epsilon=0.85?

The random branch can select A too. Exploring and choosing a nongreedy arm are different events.

The probability labels P(A), P(B), and P(C) refer to choosing those arm identities at the current decision, not their success probabilities. The unique-greedy-arm condition matters. With several tied best arms and random tie breaking, the exploitation mass is divided among them. Upper Confidence Bound

Upper confidence bound (UCB) adds an exploration bonus to the estimated reward, favoring arms that may be better than limited data suggests:

A_t=\arg\max_a\left[Q_t(a)+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_t(a)}}\right].

  • \arg\max returns an arm with the largest score.
  • t is the current round, N_t(a) its earlier pull count, and \ln the natural logarithm.
  • c>0 controls bonus strength. We use c=1 for the calculations.

Try every arm once before using the formula, so every denominator is positive.

The bonus rewards uncertainty. The total is a selection score, not a probability or a guaranteed confidence percentage.

The classic UCB1 rule for rewards bounded in [0,1] corresponds to c=sqrt(2) in this parameterization. Our c=1 exercise illustrates the index calculation, not that exact theorem or a calibrated 95-percent interval. For fixed t the bonus decreases as N grows. If an arm is ignored, its count stays fixed while ln(t) grows. Reference: Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer (2002), https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1013689704352, and the authors’ exposition at https://www.banditalgs.com/2016/09/18/the-upper-confidence-bound-algorithm/.

Updating the Chosen Arm

Suppose the chosen video C now receives reward R_{11}=1. Only its count and mean change.

For the chosen arm a=A_t, the incremental update computes the same average without storing all past ratings:

N_{t+1}(a)=N_t(a)+1,\qquad Q_{t+1}(a)=Q_t(a)+\frac{R_t-Q_t(a)}{N_t(a)+1}.

N_{12}(C)=2 and Q_{12}(C)=0+(1-0)/2=0.5. The statistics for A and B stay unchanged.

Counts are defined before round t throughout the lecture. The denominator uses the new count N_t(a)+1, not the old count. For a first observation, this rule with initial Q=0 and N=0 sets Q to the observed reward. The new reward 1 is a stipulated possible outcome, not a claim that choosing C guarantees success. C’s actual two observed rewards are now 0,1. This formula is a sample-average update, not the constant-step-size variant for nonstationary problems.

Regret: Reward Missed While Learning

The best mean is \mu^*=\max_a\mu_a. Expected regret compares a learner with always choosing an arm having that best mean:

\operatorname{Regret}(T)=T\mu^*-\mathbb E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_t\right].

Here T is the number of rounds, and \mathbb E averages over random rewards and action choices.

  • A pull of arm a loses \mu^*-\mu_a expected reward relative to the benchmark.
  • A learner pays for poor choices along the way, even if it identifies the best arm at the end.

The true means are usually unknown to the learner. A simulator can reveal them to an evaluator without giving them to the algorithm.

This is expected pseudo-regret for the stationary stochastic model, not regret against the best noisy realized reward sequence. With counts defined before each round, the equivalent decomposition is sum_a (mu-mu_a) E[N_{T+1}(a)]. We omit that extra notation from the slide. A single realized reward total can exceed Tmu* through luck; that does not contradict nonnegative expected regret. The benchmark is an evaluator’s reference, not a resource the learner has access to.

Two Different Learning Costs

For evaluation, suppose hidden true means are (\mu_A,\mu_B,\mu_C)=(0.4,0.6,0.8). Always choosing C would give 100\cdot0.8=80 expected reward over 100 rounds.

For the first allocation: 50\cdot0.4+30\cdot0.6+20\cdot0.8=54, so expected regret is 80-54=26.

Both allocations might finish by recommending C, yet they missed different amounts of reward along the way.

These are hypothetical fixed 100-pull allocations, not simulations or measured results from epsilon-greedy/UCB. They are not continuations of the exact ten-round history used in the action-selection walkthrough. All figures concern the same three resource identities and an illustrative stationary world. The second allocation gives .420+.620+.8*60=68 expected reward and regret 12. The true means here differ from the noisy empirical estimates earlier; the learner has not been allowed to use them.

Comparing Bandit Algorithms

Use the same arm distributions and horizon T for every algorithm.

  • Independent runs restart learning with fresh random outcomes.
  • Compare cumulative reward or regret and the variation across runs.
  • Keep the true means available only to the evaluator.

Compare performance across runs, not one lucky run or only the final recommendation.

The diagram is an evaluation procedure, not an empirical performance comparison. Independent runs quantify how sensitive results are to stochastic rewards and algorithm randomness. Paired common random numbers can be a useful experimental design, but are not required for this introduction. No real application reveals all unchosen rewards or the true arm means merely because we wish to evaluate a bandit algorithm.

When the Basic Model Is Too Simple

New term Meaning Resource example
Nonstationary bandit Reward distributions change over time. A video becomes outdated after a library update.
Contextual bandit Observe features before choosing, then learn which actions work for that context. Beginners and experienced students may prefer different formats.

Changing rewards can require more emphasis on recent feedback. Context-dependent choices can require separate predictions for different requests.

If a recommendation changes a student’s future knowledge and rewards, a one-step bandit omits that sequential effect. Full reinforcement learning (RL) can model action-dependent future states.

Nonstationarity and context are different extensions: a contextual problem can be stationary conditional on the context. The table supplies names and motivation, not a new algorithm or a claim that stationary UCB guarantees automatically apply. A contextual bandit ordinarily treats the context sequence as outside the learner’s action-dependent state dynamics. If actions influence future contexts, the one-step abstraction can miss long-term consequences. More recent feedback can be emphasized with a constant-step-size estimate, deferred to a context where that update is needed.

Quick Check

  1. Four arms, \epsilon=0.2, and one unique greedy arm: what is its selection probability?
  2. Does one zero reward prove that an arm’s true mean is zero?
  3. Is a UCB score of 1.4 invalid because reward is only 0 or 1?

1. 0.85. Exploitation gives 0.8, and exploration adds 0.2/4=0.05.

2. No. One observation is evidence about a distribution, not its true mean.

3. No. UCB compares estimate-plus-bonus scores. Those scores need not lie in [0,1].

Takeaways

  • An arm is a choice, and a pull reveals only that choice’s reward.
  • Sample averages estimate unknown reward means.
  • Exploitation uses current estimates. Exploration gathers more evidence.
  • Epsilon-greedy explores uniformly with probability \epsilon.
  • UCB adds a bonus that reflects limited sampling.
  • Regret includes the reward missed throughout learning.

Keep observed rewards, estimated means, unknown true means, and selection scores distinct.
