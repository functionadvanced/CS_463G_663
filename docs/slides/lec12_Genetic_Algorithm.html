Population-Based Search

A genetic algorithm (GA) searches with a population of candidate solutions. A generation is one round of reproduction and replacement.

  • Selection chooses parents using solution quality.
  • Crossover combines parents; mutation introduces random changes.

Unlike the single-state methods in Lecture 11, a GA keeps several candidates. More candidates do not guarantee a solution.

A candidate is often called an individual. The analogy to biological evolution motivates inheritance, selection, and variation, but a GA is a designed optimization procedure, not a literal model of natural evolution. Fitness is the task-specific score, introduced explicitly on the next two slides. The population is a collection that may contain duplicate candidates, not necessarily a mathematical set. This lecture develops one eight-queens example before connecting to the permutation representation used by the existing notebook. Source: AIMA genetic algorithms and the original Lecture 12 Beamer material.

Encoding an 8-Queens Board

Place eight queens so that no pair shares a row, column, or diagonal. Let x_i be the row of the queen in column i.

A gene is one entry x_i; a chromosome is the full candidate x=(x_1,\ldots,x_8).

One queen per column is automatic. Repeated rows are allowed in a candidate.

Rows and columns are numbered 1 through 8, with row 1 at the top. Every sequence of eight legal row values represents a board with one queen per column, even when the board is not a solution. There are 8^8 such encodings. The gene at column 6 has value 5; the entry is a row number, not a fitness value or tile label. Later we will impose a permutation constraint, which changes both the set of valid chromosomes and the operators. Source: the AIMA chromosome 24748552 carried over from the Spring 2025 lecture. Fitness: Count Non-Attacking Pairs

There are \binom{8}{2}=28 unordered queen pairs. Let c(x) count attacking pairs; maximize fitness F(x)=28-c(x).

Is F(x)=24 a solution? No. All 28 pairs must be non-attacking.

For columns i<j, queens attack when x_i=x_j or |x_i-x_j|=j-i. Column conflicts are impossible under this encoding. The four attacking pairs in this example are (1,8), (2,4), (3,8), and (6,7), so the fitness is 24. Fitness counts pairs, not the number of correctly placed queens. A board can have high fitness without any queen being in an independently correct final location. Minimizing conflict count and maximizing non-attacking pairs rank these boards identically. For n queens, the target is n(n-1)/2. Source: AIMA’s eight-queens genetic algorithm example; pair enumeration checked for this diagram.

For the first selected pair B and A, choose a cut after gene 3. A child, or offspring, inherits a prefix from one parent and a suffix from the other.

Child 1: B’s prefix + A’s suffix.

Child 2 uses the other two segments. A valid encoding is preserved, but fitness need not improve.

Mutation: Change a Gene

In this encoding, each gene may independently change to another row. For example, use mutation probability p_{\text{gene}}=0.10.

This mutation makes the child worse. Mutation introduces variation; it does not promise improvement.

The example changes only gene 6 in child 1 from row 5 to row 6. All other genes and the other three children remain unchanged in our worked generation. The chromosome becomes 32748652 and fitness drops from 23 to 21. Choosing a different replacement row would produce a different score; mutation samples a change instead of testing all changes and taking the best. With independent probability 0.10 per gene, a child may have zero, one, or multiple mutations. This is different from a probability of mutating the child once. Select the new row from the other seven values if a triggered mutation is required to actually change the gene. Replacement and Elitism

Cross B and C at the same cut. Of the four children, mutate only child 1 from B and A.

How can we keep the best fitness from dropping? Elitism: copy the best candidate unchanged, then fill the remaining slots with children.

Keeping one elite protects the best score, not the average. Keep the population size fixed.

This figure completes the same generation used on the selection, crossover, and mutation slides. The selected pairs B,A and B,C are both cut after gene 3. Only child 32748552 mutates, becoming 32748652. The offspring fitness values are consequently 21,22,22,18. The original best chromosome A has fitness 24 and is absent from those four children. In a size-four population with one elite, copy A unchanged and generate or retain only three children. This is a variant of full generational replacement, not an extra fifth individual. Protect an independent copy from later mutation. A separate best-ever record can preserve the returned answer even when the population itself loses it; elitism additionally keeps that candidate in the population. Source: original course continuation of the AIMA example and the notebook’s one-elite policy.

Genetic Algorithm Pseudocode

N is population size; G is the generation budget. EVALUATE assigns fitness F. This version keeps one elite.

GENETIC(N, G, target)
P <- RANDOM_POP(N); EVALUATE(P)
best <- COPY(ARGMAX_F(P))
for generation in 1 .. G:
if F(best) = target: break
next <- [COPY(ARGMAX_F(P))]
while SIZE(next) < N:
a, b <- SELECT(P), SELECT(P)
child <- MUTATE(CROSSOVER(a, b))
APPEND(next, child)
P <- next; EVALUATE(P)
if MAX_F(P) > F(best): best <- COPY(ARGMAX_F(P))
return best

ARGMAX_F returns a highest-fitness candidate; MAX_F returns its score. For eight queens, target = 28.

P and next are populations; a and b are parents; child is a newly created chromosome. COPY makes an independent snapshot. N should be at least two for one-elite reproduction to produce new candidates. SELECT implements a stated selection rule, and CROSSOVER and MUTATE must preserve the chosen encoding without altering the parents. Mutation parameters can be passed to MUTATE or stored as configuration. All individuals are evaluated before comparison, including those produced in the last allowed generation. Returning best does not mean target was reached. With valid elitism, the best in P is already a best-ever candidate; the explicit best variable makes the return contract clear and remains useful in variants without elitism. Source: course pseudocode based on AIMA and the existing notebook, not a transcription of its implementation.

A Different Encoding: Permutations

Require each row number to appear exactly once. A permutation then prevents both row and column conflicts; only diagonals remain.

Operators must preserve the encoding. The notebook uses a permutation-preserving crossover, not ordinary one-point crossover.

The four-gene example is only an encoding check, not a claimed four-queens solution. Repeated rows were allowed under the earlier row-vector encoding, so 1212 was an encodable candidate there; it is invalid specifically under the permutation encoding. For eight queens there are 8! permutations instead of 8^8 unrestricted row vectors. The existing genetic_8_queen notebook uses random.sample(range(n),n) to create permutations and currently sets n=30. It also changes selection: tournament selection samples a group and chooses its highest-fitness member. The notebook samples ten candidates per tournament. That rule does not divide fitness by the population’s total. Source: existing genetic_8_queen.ipynb and standard representation constraints.

Mutation Depends on the Encoding

A permutation cannot freely replace one value: it could create a duplicate. Swap mutation exchanges two positions instead.

Row-vector example Permutation notebook
0.10 chance per gene to replace its row 0.30 chance per child to swap two entries
Several genes may change independently At most one swap per child

These rates describe different events. A swap preserves the permutation, but may improve or worsen fitness.

For this child, swapping the values at human columns 2 and 6 changes fitness from 25 to 26. Both positions must be distinct. The notebook’s mutate function draws one random value to decide whether to swap, then samples two positions. Thus mutation_rate=0.3 does not mean that each queen mutates with probability 0.3. By contrast, independent per-gene probability 0.1 on eight genes gives probability 1-0.9^8 of at least one triggered change, about 0.57. Parameter values cannot be compared meaningfully without knowing their events. Source: original lecture’s per-gene mutation and existing notebook’s swap mutation.

Diversity and Stopping

Diversity means having different chromosomes in the population. Strong selection can make the population increasingly similar.

Can crossover alone create a new chromosome from identical parents? No. Mutation can introduce variation that is missing from the population.

  • Stop at the target fitness or a time / generation budget.
  • Keep the best candidate; report whether it actually reached the target.
  • Compare several random seeds, including unsuccessful runs.

Premature convergence means losing diversity and concentrating around candidates before finding a sufficiently good solution. Mutation can help, but does not guarantee escape or success in a finite run. Excessive mutation can also disrupt useful combinations. Elitism preserves a maximum score but does not force average fitness to increase. Tournament size and fitness scaling affect selection pressure; larger tournaments generally favor the strongest candidates more aggressively. A generation limit is a resource limit, not evidence that the problem has no solution. Source: AIMA discussion of genetic search and the notebook’s exploration questions.
