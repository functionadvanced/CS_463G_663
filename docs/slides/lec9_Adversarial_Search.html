Tic-Tac-Toe

Tic-tac-toe is a two-player game on a 3\times3 board.

  • X starts. Players alternate, marking one empty square per turn.
  • The first player with three marks in a row, column, or diagonal wins.
  • A full board with no winner is a draw.

We choose X’s moves. Who chooses O’s replies?

The opponent controls O. Our plan must account for their choices, including moves that block our win.

Explain the rules before analyzing the position. Each player keeps the same symbol throughout the game. The board shown is a position partway through play; the small numbers label its empty squares and are not scores. A winning line ends the game immediately. Contrast with UCS and A*: in deterministic route finding, the agent controls every action along its chosen plan. Here we choose X’s moves but cannot choose O’s replies. A sequence that leads to a win is useful only if the opponent cannot avoid it. The next slide introduces the decision rule for handling those replies. Source: AIMA adversarial search chapter.

Minimax: The Basic Idea

Minimax chooses the move with the best worst-case outcome, accounting for an opponent who tries to make our outcome as bad as possible.

Give each outcome a utility, a numerical score from our viewpoint:

  • MAX is our player, seeking the highest score.
  • MIN is the opponent, seeking the lowest score for us.

For now, both players see the entire state, moves have no random effects, and every game eventually ends.

For each candidate move, consider the worst reply. Then choose the move whose worst outcome is best.

MAX and MIN abbreviate maximizing and minimizing. We use one numerical utility measured from MAX’s viewpoint; MIN wants the opposite outcome. This is a zero-sum model. Seeing the entire state is called perfect information, and known move results without randomness are deterministic. The rule considers replies throughout the remaining game, not just the opponent’s next move. Exact recursive minimax here assumes a finite game tree with two alternating players. Games with dice, hidden information, or shared objectives require extensions. Source: AIMA adversarial search chapter.

Utility from MAX’s Viewpoint

  • MAX = X: prefer +1 to 0 to -1.
  • MIN = O: prefer -1 to 0 to +1.
  • The score’s meaning stays the same at every level.

At an O turn, choose the smallest X score. Do not change the sign just because the turn changes.

In a zero-sum model, O’s own utility is the negative of X’s. We can therefore store only X’s score. Terminal scores are stipulated by the rules; the values3,12,8 in the abstract example use a different utility scale. Do not add edge costs as in UCS or A*. A utility of0 here means an actual draw, not an unevaluated state.

Tic-Tac-Toe Game Tree

Each edge is one player’s move, or one ply. A terminal branch has a score. Other branches continue with the opponent’s turn.

Ask why the middle branch stops before O moves. Other branches continue even though their descendants are omitted in this sketch; they must not be treated as terminal. The top layer is MAX’s decision; a surviving child is MIN’s turn. Branches can end at different depths. A two-ply search includes one X move and one O reply, unless a terminal state is reached sooner. Tic-tac-toe ends after at most nine plies from the empty board. The Chosen Move and Its Guarantee

Result Meaning
Value: 3 Best worst-case payoff
Action: A Move to play now

Choosing A guarantees at least 3. If MIN selects 12 instead, MAX gets 12.

After each opponent reply, choose again from the resulting state. A strategy specifies how to act in every position that may arise.

With perfect play by both players the outcome is exactly 3. Against any legal opponent, MAX can guarantee at least 3 by following a minimax strategy. Minimax protects against the strongest replies without predicting that every real opponent will be optimal. The highlighted line is one possible optimal continuation, not a fixed plan the opponent must follow. In a longer game, recompute after each observed reply or store a contingent strategy. The Minimax Rule

  • s: a state; a: an action; A(s): the legal actions in s.
  • \operatorname{Result}(s,a): the next state after action a in state s.
  • U(s): terminal score; V(s): score from s under optimal play.

V(s)= \begin{cases} U(s), & \text{if }s\text{ is terminal},\\[0.35em] \max\limits_{a\in A(s)} V(\operatorname{Result}(s,a)), & \text{if MAX moves},\\[0.35em] \min\limits_{a\in A(s)} V(\operatorname{Result}(s,a)), & \text{if MIN moves}. \end{cases}

For MAX: \arg\max gives the action(s) with the largest child value. Choose one, a^*:

a^*\in\arg\max_{a\in A(s)}V(\operatorname{Result}(s,a)).

Read Result(s,a) as “the state reached after taking action a in state s.” It is the same operation as RESULT in the game representation and pseudocode. A(s) abbreviates ACTIONS(s), and U(s) abbreviates UTILITY(s). V(Result(s,a)) is therefore the value of a child state. The recurrence computes the score, whereas arg max identifies actions achieving the largest score. The symbol in between a* and arg max means “is one of,” allowing multiple equally good actions. The action-selection formula assumes MAX moves; use arg min at MIN’s turn. The same fixed MAX viewpoint applies throughout. Quick check: MAX with children -7, -2, -5 has value -2. Source: AIMA adversarial search chapter and Berkeley CS188 minimax notes.

Why Minimax Is Correct

Assume a finite tree, correct game rules, and exact terminal utilities.

  1. At a leaf: the utility is the actual outcome.
  2. At a MAX node: if the child values are correct, MAX can choose the largest.
  3. At a MIN node: MIN can choose the smallest, so MAX cannot count on anything larger.

Repeat the argument from the leaves to the root. Each backed-up value is correct once its children are correct.

Following minimax guarantees MAX at least the root value. This may still mean a win, draw, or loss against optimal play.

This is induction on subtree height. At MAX nodes there exists an action achieving the backed-up bound. At MIN nodes every legal reply has value at least the minimum, and an optimal MIN can attain that minimum. With perfect play by both sides the outcome equals the root value. For tic-tac-toe, exact value 0 means a draw under optimal play, not an unfinished calculation. The theorem relies on actual terminal utilities; arbitrary heuristic cutoffs lose the guarantee.

Minimax Pseudocode: Evaluate a State

VALUE(s) evaluates the child states, then returns their maximum if MAX moves or minimum if MIN moves.

VALUE(s)
if TERMINAL(s): return UTILITY(s)
if PLAYER(s) == MAX:
v <- -infinity
for a in ACTIONS(s):
v <- max(v, VALUE(RESULT(s, a)))
else: # MIN moves
v <- +infinity
for a in ACTIONS(s):
v <- min(v, VALUE(RESULT(s, a)))
return v

This child-to-parent calculation is a value backup. Search depth first, then return the parent’s value.

No depth parameter appears in exact VALUE: the stopping condition is terminal. The code assumes a finite game tree and at least one legal action in every nonterminal state. For a game with passing, a pass must be represented as a legal action. Terminal utility always refers to MAX. The loop accumulators use opposite infinities so they also handle all-negative MAX values or all-positive MIN values correctly. Source: standard minimax recurrence, rewritten as course pseudocode.

Minimax Pseudocode: Choose an Action

Precondition: the state is nonterminal and MAX moves next.

CHOOSE_MOVE(s)
best_value <- -infinity; best_action <- None
for a in ACTIONS(s):
value <- VALUE(RESULT(s, a))
if value > best_value:
best_value <- value
best_action <- a
return best_action

Whenever a better score appears, store its action too. With >, a tie keeps the first move found.

The recursive function computes scores. The outer loop remembers the move that produced the best score.

The strict comparison retains the first action among equally valued alternatives. The successor of a MAX move normally belongs to MIN; the state carries that turn, so the caller need not toggle a separate flag. This is the counterpart of retaining a parent/action in UCS and A*: computing a numeric value alone is insufficient to act. Standard tic-tac-toe utilities are finite.

Search Cost

Exact search works for tic-tac-toe. For larger games, let b be the branching factor and m the maximum remaining plies.

Time

A full tree has

1+b+\cdots+b^m=O(b^m).

For b=3, m=10: 59,049 leaves and 88,573 total nodes.

Memory

Depth-first evaluation keeps the active recursion path and pending siblings.

  • O(bm) if each call stores its children.
  • O(m) if successors are generated one at a time.

You can evaluate a tree without storing all of it. The number of positions examined still grows exponentially.

These are search-node/record counts with state representation costs treated as fixed. The O(m) version assumes a constant-size successor iterator and no table retaining prior states. Eagerly storing the entire tree would instead take exponential memory. The full-tree formula assumes b greater than1; b=3 is a constructed branching example, not a claim about all tic-tac-toe positions. Tic-tac-toe branching decreases as squares fill, and some games end early. Standard depth-first minimax complexity follows AIMA.

Terminal Utility and Cutoff Evaluation

Large games may require stopping before a terminal state. Let d count plies still available.

VALUE_LIMITED(s, d):
    if TERMINAL(s): return UTILITY(s)
    if d == 0:     return EVAL(s)
    # Otherwise use the same MAX / MIN recursion,
    # calling VALUE_LIMITED(RESULT(s, a), d - 1).
Leaf type Returned score
Terminal position Exact outcome U(s)
Nonterminal depth cutoff Estimated value \operatorname{EVAL}(s)

Unlike A*’s remaining-cost estimate, EVAL estimates game value. Heuristic errors can change the move; optimal play is no longer guaranteed.

Check TERMINAL before d==0 so a terminal state at the cutoff receives its exact utility. EVAL always uses the same MAX viewpoint. Minimax over heuristic leaves is exact for those supplied leaf scores but is not guaranteed optimal in the actual game. Unlike A*’s admissible remaining-cost heuristic, there is no admissibility condition introduced here that restores exact play. Takeaways

  • A game state includes whose turn it is.
  • MAX takes the maximum; MIN takes the minimum, using one utility viewpoint.
  • Back up values, then retain the action that achieves the root’s best value.
  • Exact minimax gives the best worst-case guarantee, but its time cost is exponential.
