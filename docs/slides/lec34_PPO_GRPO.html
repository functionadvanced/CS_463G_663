Proximal Policy Optimization

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) improves a policy using sampled experience. “Proximal” means nearby: the update objective discourages large changes from the policy that collected the data.

Recall: \pi_\theta(a\mid s) is action a’s probability in state s, with policy weights \theta. An advantage estimate \hat A compares its return with a state-value baseline.

John Schulman et al. (2017), Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms. We study the PPO-Clip form.

PPO is a family that also includes a KL-penalty formulation. This lecture focuses on the clipped surrogate. Proximal is motivation, not a hard distance guarantee. The actor has parameters theta; a standard actor-critic implementation also learns a value predictor. Advantage can be estimated by a complete discounted return minus a learned state value, or by multi-step TD methods. The supplied PPO Beamer material and the original paper provide the starting point. Public-domain robot: https://openclipart.org/detail/325644/toy-robot. Implementation background and clipping caveats: https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/algorithms/ppo.html.

The New-to-Old Probability Ratio

The old policy \pi_{\text{old}} generated the current batch, a set of collected examples. Freeze it: hold its parameters fixed during updates to this batch.

\rho_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.

For pickup, \rho=0.65/0.50=1.30. For the small job, \rho=0.35/0.50=0.70.

The delivery returns are 6.2 and 4. Using baseline 5.1 gives advantages +1.1 for pickup and -1.1 for small.

This reuses Lecture 33’s fictional delivery choice: pickup incurs -1 then delivery gives 8 with discount .9, while the small job immediately gives 4. The old policy chooses both with probability .5, so its exact state-value baseline is 5.1. These are stipulated examples, not measured learning results. The two candidate probabilities form the same normalized policy; positive and negative clipping examples below use this one candidate. Rho is a probability ratio, distinct from a reward r. The sampled action and state remain fixed while evaluating the candidate probability. Old probabilities must be positive for sampled actions.

The Clipped Score

The clipping parameter \epsilon sets a ratio band. Use \epsilon=0.2, giving [0.8,1.2].

\operatorname{clip}(x,l,u) replaces values below l by l, and values above u by u.

\ell(\rho,\hat A)=\min\left\{\rho\hat A,\; \operatorname{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A\right\}.

PPO maximizes the average of these scores over collected examples. The advantage estimate stays fixed during this batch’s policy updates.

The minimum makes the objective conservative. The flat region depends on whether the advantage is positive or negative.

This is the standard PPO-Clip empirical surrogate, not a claim that its minibatch gradient is identical to an exact discounted-start policy gradient at every parameter value. We use a per-sample score ell and average it over data; implementations also train a critic and may add entropy bonuses or other safeguards. The teaching value epsilon=.2 is not a universal best setting. Clip applies inside the objective. It does not directly project the actual action probabilities into a permitted interval. Source: Schulman et al. (2017), Equation 7, and the supplied Beamer clipping frame.

Positive Advantage: Pickup

Pickup has \hat A=+1.1. Its probability rises from 0.50 to 0.65, so \rho=1.30 lies above the upper band edge 1.2.

\ell=\min\{1.30\cdot1.1,\;1.20\cdot1.1\}=1.32.

Further increasing this ratio adds no benefit to this sample’s clipped score. The actual probability can still be 0.65; clipping does not force it to 0.60.

Raw score is 1.43 and the clipped alternative is 1.32. For positive advantage the plateau begins at 1+epsilon, not at both band edges. Ratios below the lower edge still have the raw, smaller score. Other samples, shared parameters, and optimizer steps can move a probability even when this particular term is flat. Neither a hard KL bound nor monotonic performance improvement follows from clipping alone. The curves are exact piecewise-linear calculations, not training curves.

Negative Advantage: Small Job

The small job has \hat A=-1.1. Under the same candidate policy, its probability falls from 0.50 to 0.35, giving \rho=0.70.

\ell=\min\{0.70(-1.1),\;0.80(-1.1)\}=-0.88.

The minimum selects -0.88, not -0.77. Reducing this bad action’s probability further brings no extra benefit to this term. Making it more likely still worsens the score.

Multiplying by a negative advantage reverses which ratio gives the smaller product. The plateau is below 1-epsilon for a negative advantage. Above that edge the objective keeps the raw negative slope, including above 1+epsilon. This is why replacing the minimum by a maximum, or describing clipping as a hard bound on probabilities, is incorrect. The positive and negative figures use the same ratio axis, epsilon, and absolute advantage. All values are constructed calculations.

One PPO Training Round

A rollout is a sampled interaction sequence. An epoch is one pass through the collected batch; a minibatch is a smaller chunk used for one optimizer step.

old = FrozenCopy(policy)
batch = CollectRollouts(old)
targets, A = EstimateReturnsAndAdvantages(batch, critic)
for K epochs, for each minibatch
ratio = ActionRatios(policy, old, minibatch)
actorLoss = -mean(ClipScore(ratio, A))
criticLoss = mean((V(s) - targets)2)
UpdateActorAndCritic(actorLoss, criticLoss)

The critic predicts state value V(s). For example, estimate advantage as discounted return minus V(s). ClipScore is \ell from the preceding slides.

Keep old, targets, and advantages fixed for these K passes. Then collect a new batch with the updated policy.

This shows the common actor-critic PPO form, not a claim that the clipped objective mathematically requires a learned critic. Stop gradients through advantage estimates, value targets, and old-policy probabilities. Each minibatch uses the matching subsets of A and targets. K is a chosen small number of passes, not permission to reuse arbitrary old data indefinitely. In practice saved old log-probabilities often replace a full duplicate old network, and ratios are exp(logp-logp_old). Learning-rate control and KL-based early stopping can provide extra safeguards. We omit GAE terminology and implementation details to keep the first presentation self-contained.

A Language Model as a Policy

A large language model (LLM) chooses text pieces called tokens. At each step, the state is the prompt plus text already generated, and the action is the next token.

Write q for the prompt and y for the completed response. Generating an answer is an episode ending when the response finishes.

A response-level score can train many token choices. Its source may be human preferences or an automatic correctness checker.

The figure uses an explicitly toy vocabulary containing the three shown text pieces; it does not claim a particular real tokenizer splits text this way. A production language model repeatedly conditions on the growing prefix and includes a stopping mechanism. We only need that sequential-policy view here, not a Transformer architecture. The numeric probabilities are hypothetical. A complete response probability factors into token probabilities, but the PPO/GRPO objectives introduced later clip token ratios individually rather than clipping one product for the whole response.

RLHF: Learning from Human Preferences

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) uses human judgments to guide policy training. Here is the classic reward-model-and-PPO pipeline:

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) continues an already trained model’s training on example answers. A reward model (RM) learns to score responses from human comparisons.

Long Ouyang et al. (2022), Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback.

RLHF is a feedback-based training pipeline. PPO is one optimizer that can be used inside it.

This is the classic InstructGPT-style pipeline, not a definition claiming every use of human preferences must include PPO or a separate learned reward model. First learn desired answer behavior from demonstrations, then collect rankings/comparisons of candidate answers, fit a reward model, and use RL to improve the policy against that model. The simplified diagram has three stages, with comparison collection included in reward-model training. We omit pretraining-data mixing and paper-specific implementation choices. Source: Ouyang et al. (2022), Sections 3.1 and 3.5, https://arxiv.org/html/2203.02155.

Fitting the Reward Model

A preference pair has the same prompt q, a human-preferred answer y^+, and a rejected answer y^-. Let s_\phi(q,y) be the reward model’s scalar score, with weights \phi.

For one pair, minimize:

L_{\text{RM}}=-\log\sigma\!\left(s_\phi(q,y^+)-s_\phi(q,y^-)\right).

The sigmoid \sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z}) converts the score difference to a modeled preference probability. Here 2-0.5=1.5 gives \sigma(1.5)\approx0.818.

This is a constructed preference example, not collected human-labeling data or outputs of a trained model. The loss is averaged over many comparison pairs in training. Sigmoid(z)=1/(1+exp(-z)); natural log is used. The model scores themselves need not be probabilities or objectively calibrated quality measurements. Their difference parameterizes the modeled probability of the observed preference. Here the negative log-likelihood is about .201413. Reward-model fitting updates phi; in the subsequent RL stage we hold its weights fixed while policy and value predictions change. Source: Ouyang et al. reward-model loss, Equation 1. A value critic predicts expected return under a policy and has a different role from this preference scorer.

Rollout Snapshot and Reference Policy

The reference policy \pi_{\text{ref}} is a fixed SFT copy during this RLHF stage. It anchors behavior. The rollout snapshot \pi_{\text{old}} is refreshed when collecting each new PPO batch.

Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence measures how one probability distribution differs from another. At a fixed text prefix, let P be the current token distribution and Q the reference:

D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)=\sum_a P(a)\log\frac{P(a)}{Q(a)}.

Optimize preference reward while penalizing this departure. A coefficient \beta\geq0 controls penalty strength. KL is zero for equal distributions and is asymmetric.

The sum ranges over token actions. Assume the reference has positive probability wherever the current policy does. KL is nonnegative in expectation/distribution form, although an individual sampled log-ratio can be negative. In language-model RLHF, a sampled token penalty uses beta*log(pi_old(token|prefix)/pi_ref(token|prefix)) when forming the fixed rollout rewards; the expected log-ratio gives a KL cost under the collection policy. A score from the reward model is added at response completion. The reference anchors the SFT behavior; the old snapshot supplies fixed denominators for recent-data importance ratios. They coincide initially but generally differ after training. PPO clipping alone does not enforce either KL bound. Source: Ouyang et al., Equation 2 and the per-token penalty discussion.

An RLHF Training Round

Start from the SFT policy and initialize a trainable value critic. Keep the learned reward model and SFT reference fixed during this RL stage.

repeat
old = FrozenCopy(policy)
batch = GenerateAnswers(old, prompts)
scores = RewardModel(prompts, batch)
rewards = ShapeRewards(batch, scores, old, reference, beta)
targets, A = EstimateReturnsAndAdvantages(batch, rewards, critic)
PPOUpdate(policy, critic, batch, old, A, targets)

ShapeRewards adds the response score at the end and subtracts sampled reference-policy penalties at generated tokens. PPOUpdate performs a bounded number of passes over this batch.

Humans provide training comparisons. The fitted reward model can then score many RL rollouts without a new human judgment at every step.

The generation routine stores token actions, prefixes, old log-probabilities, and termination information, not just printable answer strings. For sampled token t, the shaped reward is minus beta times the old-to-reference log-probability ratio, with the learned response score added on the final token. Targets and advantages are detached and fixed during the PPO inner updates. The value critic learns returns from these shaped rewards. The reference and reward-model parameters receive no RL-stage gradient here. This is an algorithmic overview of the classic pipeline, not runnable library code. Refitting a reward model from new human data would be a separate outer stage.

Direct Preference Optimization

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains the policy directly from chosen/rejected answer pairs, without fitting a separate reward model or running a PPO rollout loop.

Reuse prompt q, preferred answer y^+, and rejected answer y^-. Offline means the comparisons are saved before this training stage.

Rafael Rafailov et al. (2023), Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model.

DPO still uses a fixed reference policy and depends on the quality of the preference pairs.

This is the original offline DPO setup. Responses are generated and labeled before the illustrated optimization stage. Online variants are outside this introduction. Source: Rafailov et al., Sections 3 and 4. The policy is initialized from SFT, with a frozen copy as the reference.

DPO Loss: A Worked Pair

Define reference-relative log-probability ratios for the two complete answers:

u_\theta^\pm=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^\pm\mid q)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^\pm\mid q)},\qquad L_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\!\left(\beta(u_\theta^+-u_\theta^-)\right).

The preference margin is u_\theta^+-u_\theta^-. The chosen \beta>0 scales it; \sigma is the sigmoid. Minimize the average pair loss.

Toy probabilities for our preferred 156 explanation and rejected 144 answer:

Model \pi(y^+\mid q) \pi(y^-\mid q)
Fixed reference 0.20 0.20
Current policy 0.40 0.10

With \beta=1, the margin is \log 2-\log 0.5=\log 4. Thus the modeled preference probability is 0.8, and L_{\text{DPO}}=-\log 0.8\approx0.223.

All probabilities are constructed, not actual model outputs. Other answers have the remaining probability mass. Natural logarithms are used. The margin is u-plus minus u-minus. At initialization policy equals reference, giving margin zero and pair loss log(2), about .693. These are whole-response ratios, unlike PPO/GRPO token-level clipping. The reference-relative loss comes from a KL-regularized preference model; it is not a hard bound on policy distance. Increasing a pair margin does not guarantee that either answer’s absolute probability changes monotonically under shared model parameters. Equation 7 of the original DPO paper gives the dataset average of this pair loss.

The DPO Training Loop

Initialize the policy from SFT. Hold the reference fixed throughout this offline training stage.

policy = Copy(SFT)
reference = FrozenCopy(SFT)
for each minibatch (q, yPlus, yMinus) of saved preferences
uPlus = ResponseLogRatio(policy, reference, q, yPlus)
uMinus = ResponseLogRatio(policy, reference, q, yMinus)
loss = -mean(log(sigmoid(beta * (uPlus - uMinus))))
OptimizerStep(policy, loss)

ResponseLogRatio returns u_\theta^\pm from the preceding slide. Score the saved answer tokens, conditioned on the prompt and their earlier tokens. Sum their log-probabilities for each complete answer, rather than sampling a new answer.

Only the policy is trained here. There is no separate reward-model training, value critic, or online rollout collection in this loop.

Sum response-token log-probabilities, not prompt-token probabilities, using consistent termination and padding masks for both models. Reference outputs are detached and can be precomputed for a fixed dataset. In code, use a numerically stable log-sigmoid instead of explicitly taking log(sigmoid(…)). The display uses the mathematical operations for clarity. This is algorithmic pseudocode, not a library API. There is no old-policy snapshot denominator and no clipping in this DPO loss.

Group Relative Policy Optimization

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) estimates learning weights from a group of G responses to the same prompt, instead of training a separate value critic.

Here G=4. A verifier, an automatic checker, gives 1 for final answer 156 and 0 otherwise. This example uses checked correctness rather than human preference scores.

Zhihong Shao et al. (2024), DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models.

The group is sampled from a frozen collection-policy snapshot. These answers and rewards are a constructed example, not DeepSeek outputs or benchmark results. The original paper studies reward models and both outcome and process supervision. We use one final-answer verifier reward per response for a minimal outcome-scored example; this choice of reward source is not the definition of GRPO. Critic-free does not mean reward-model-free: GRPO can also use a learned scorer. Its relative weights are not exact state-action advantages. Source: DeepSeekMath Section 4.1, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.03300.

Group-Relative Learning Weights

For response rewards r_1,\ldots,r_G, use group mean \mu and population standard deviation \sigma_r, which measures within-group spread:

\mu=\operatorname{mean}_i r_i,\qquad \sigma_r=\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}_i[(r_i-\mu)^2]}.

The group-relative advantage estimate is

\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\mu}{\sigma_r}\quad\text{when }\sigma_r>0.

For (r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4)=(1,0,1,0), \mu=0.5 and \sigma_r=0.5.

Thus (\hat A_1,\hat A_2,\hat A_3,\hat A_4)=(1,-1,1,-1).

Equal rewards give zero spread and no relative reward signal. In our pseudocode, return zero weights for that group instead of dividing by zero.

Population standard deviation, with divisor G, is chosen explicitly for the arithmetic; software conventions must match the stated calculation. Implementations may instead use a numerical stabilizer or skip zero-variance groups. Here NormalizeOrZero returns all zeros exactly when sigma is zero. A KL regularization term can still produce a policy update in that case, so only the relative reward component vanishes. The group mean includes the current response and the random standard deviation rescales it; do not infer the unbiased action-independent-baseline identity from Lecture 33 for this normalized proxy. Above/below group mean is a relative comparison, not proof of absolute quality.

GRPO Uses Token-Level Ratios

For response i, let y_{i,t} be token t and y_{i,<t} the earlier tokens. The state s_{i,t} contains prompt q and that prefix.

\rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})} {\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})}.

Use the same response weight \hat A_i for its tokens in this final-reward example. Average the PPO-style scores \ell(\rho_{i,t},\hat A_i) within each response, then across the group.

For a token in a response with \hat A_i=1, changing its probability from 0.50 to 0.65 gives \rho=1.3 and clipped score 1.2.

Clipping uses one ratio per generated token. The original GRPO objective adds a reference-policy KL penalty separately from the group-normalized reward.

This is the outcome-supervision form, where one final normalized score weights every token in a response. The original paper also considers process rewards, which give different per-token weights. The displayed ratio conditions on the full same prefix in numerator and denominator. It is not the product of ratios across a whole response. DeepSeekMath averages over tokens within each response and then responses, with a separate KL regularizer. Its sampled KL estimator uses u-log(u)-1 with u=pi_ref/pi_theta; the reference in that term is distinct from the old policy in the clipped ratio. A reference may be refreshed between outer training stages in the paper’s iterative variant; it stays fixed within the optimization stage illustrated here.

One GRPO Training Round

Fix a reference policy and a scoring rule for this stage. Score each response with a verifier or a frozen learned reward model.

old = FrozenCopy(policy)
groups = GenerateGroups(old, prompts, G)
rewards = ScoreResponses(groups)
A = NormalizeOrZero(rewards) // separately per prompt
for K update passes
ratio = TokenRatios(policy, old, groups)
gain = MeanByResponse(ClipScore(ratio, A))
penalty = ReferenceKL(policy, reference, groups)
loss = -mean(gain) + beta * penalty
OptimizerStep(policy, loss)

MeanByResponse averages token scores within each answer. Keep the old probabilities and group weights fixed across the K passes. Only the policy is trained here.

GenerateGroups stores tokenized responses, their prefixes, and collection-policy log-probabilities. ScoreResponses may be a verifier or a reward-model inference call. NormalizeOrZero operates independently within each prompt group, never across unrelated prompts, and its output is detached during policy optimization. ClipScore is the same ell already defined. ReferenceKL denotes the separate reference regularizer, using the corresponding per-token estimation and response averaging. The illustrated stage has no value-critic loss or learned value baseline. A full outer procedure can collect new groups, refresh a reference, or retrain a scorer in separate stages. The graph and pseudocode are an introductory outcome-scored GRPO presentation, not a claim that all later GRPO variants are identical.

Reward Quality Still Matters

Prompt: “What is 12\times13? Explain briefly.”

Response Correct? Explanation? Verifier reward
12\times10+12\times3=156 Yes Yes 1
156 Yes No 1

The checker rewards both equally because it tests only the final answer.

Reward hacking means exploiting a scoring rule while missing the intended goal. A learned preference scorer can also miss important aspects of quality.

Evaluate on held-out tasks, tasks not used in training, and check the behavior humans actually wanted. A rising training reward alone is insufficient evidence.

This is a deliberately limited verifier specification, not evidence that an actual model learned a reward exploit. It illustrates an omission an optimizer could exploit and connects to the reward-hacking/specification-gaming discussion earlier in the course. Correct final answers alone do not establish that a written explanation is faithful or that the model followed every instruction. Human comparisons can contain disagreement, coverage gaps, or bias; a reward model is a proxy for those collected judgments. We make no claim that RLHF guarantees truthfulness or that GRPO guarantees sound reasoning. Background: the limitations discussion in Ouyang et al. and the reward-function discussion in DeepSeekMath.

Quick Check

  1. If \pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)=0.5 and the candidate gives 0.7, does PPO clipping force the probability back to 0.6 when \epsilon=0.2?
  2. Does “GRPO has no value critic” imply it needs no reward model?
  3. What if a group receives rewards (1,1,1,1)?

1. No. The ratio is 1.4. Clipping changes the score’s incentive, not the actual probability directly.

2. No. The scorer may still be a learned reward model, or it may be an automatic verifier.

3. All relative weights are zero under our guard. The reward component has no group contrast; a separate KL penalty may still act.

Takeaways

  • PPO-Clip limits incentives for large favorable ratio changes, without a hard policy-distance guarantee.
  • The old policy supplies fixed denominators for a recent batch.
  • Classic RLHF fits a preference reward model, then improves a policy using it.
  • DPO directly fits saved preference pairs using a reference-relative loss, without a separate reward model or RL rollout loop.
  • Rollout snapshots, fixed references, reward models, and value critics have different roles.
  • GRPO replaces the learned value baseline with group-relative reward weights.
  • Equal-score groups and incomplete reward specifications need explicit handling.
