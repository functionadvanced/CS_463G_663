Outcomes, Events, and Probability

Roll one fair six-sided die: each of its six outcomes has probability 1/6.

Term Meaning Die example
Outcome One possible result 4
Sample space \Omega All possible outcomes \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
Event A A set of outcomes Even: A=\{2,4,6\}
Probability measure P A number assigned to an event P(A)=3/6=1/2

The diagram also labels B as the event that the result is at least 4. An event occurs when the observed outcome belongs to its set. The event space F is the collection of allowed events; for this finite example, use every subset of Omega. The formal probability space is (Omega, F, P).

Counting outcomes works here because the die is fair. For unequal outcome probabilities, add their probability weights rather than dividing counts.

Probability Rules

An axiom is a starting rule. The basic axioms require nonnegative probabilities, P(\Omega)=1, and additivity for disjoint events.

Rule Meaning
0\le P(A)\le1 Every event has a valid probability
P(A^c)=1-P(A) A^c is the complement: outcomes outside A
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B) if A\cap B=\varnothing Disjoint events have no shared outcomes
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) Otherwise, subtract the overlap counted twice

\cup means or, \cap means and, and \varnothing is the empty set.

For the die, even A and at-least-4 B overlap at 4,6:

P(A\cup B)=\frac36+\frac36-\frac26=\frac46.

The upper bound 1 and complement rule follow from the axioms. General additivity applies to countably many pairwise disjoint events. The finite examples here avoid measure-theory details. The union is {2,4,5,6}, checking the arithmetic by counting.

[Sources] - AIMA Section 12.2 and Appendix A.3. [/Sources]

Conditional Probability

Conditional probability P(A\mid B) means the probability of A given that B occurred. The bar \mid means “given.”

P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.

Die example: A is even, and B is at least 4.

Two of the three remaining outcomes are even: P(A\mid B)=(2/6)/(3/6)=2/3, compared with P(A)=1/2.

Conditioning restricts the possible outcomes and rescales their probabilities to total 1. The outcomes remain equally likely in this die example. For a general model, retain relative weights rather than assuming uniformity after conditioning.

[Sources] - AIMA Section 12.2, conditional probability. [/Sources]

Independence

Events are independent when observing one leaves the other’s probability unchanged:

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).

If P(B)>0, this is equivalent to P(A\mid B)=P(A).

Draw one card uniformly from a standard 52-card deck, without jokers. Let A mean king and B mean heart.

P(A)=\frac4{52},\qquad P(B)=\frac{13}{52},\qquad P(A\cap B)=\frac1{52}.

P(A\mid B)=\frac{1/52}{13/52}=\frac1{13}=P(A).

King and heart are independent, although they overlap. Disjoint positive-probability events cannot be independent: learning one rules out the other.

The king of hearts belongs to both events. This contrasts with the die events, where conditioning changed 1/2 to 2/3. An equally valid check multiplies (4/52)(13/52)=1/52. Drawing multiple cards without replacement introduces dependencies between draws.

Random Variables and a PMF

A random variable maps each outcome to a value. For two independent fair coin tosses (heads probability 1/2), let X count heads. H means heads and T tails.

A discrete variable has countably many values. Its probability mass function (PMF) gives p_X(x)=P(X=x) for each value x.

Value x 0 1 2
p_X(x) 1/4 1/2 1/4

Uppercase X names the variable. Lowercase x names a possible value. Multiple outcomes can map to the same value.

Discrete means the possible values are countable, including finite sets such as {0,1,2}. HT and TH are separate ordered outcomes, so the head-count distribution is not uniform. The PMF entries are nonnegative and sum to 1.

[Sources] - AIMA Section 12.2 and Appendix A.3. Original two-toss diagram and table. [/Sources]

Mean and Variance

The expectation or mean is a probability-weighted average:

\mu=E[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x).

The variance measures spread using squared distances from the mean:

\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2].

For our head count, the PMF is (1/4,1/2,1/4) at x=0,1,2.

\begin{aligned} E[X]&=0\left(\frac14\right)+1\left(\frac12\right)+2\left(\frac14\right)=1,\\ \operatorname{Var}(X)&=(0-1)^2\left(\frac14\right)+(1-1)^2\left(\frac12\right)+(2-1)^2\left(\frac14\right)=\frac12. \end{aligned}

An expected value is an average, not a guaranteed outcome. A fair die has mean 3.5, even though no roll produces 3.5.

The sum runs over every possible value of X. Variance uses squared units, and standard deviation is its square root. Var(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2 gives 1.5-1=0.5 here.

[Sources] - AIMA Appendix A.3 and Stanford CS229 probability review, Sections 2.4-2.5. [/Sources]

Linearity of Expectation

Linearity means that the expectation of a sum equals the sum of the expectations:

E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y].

Let X be 1 if the first fair coin toss is heads and 0 otherwise. Define Y the same way for the second toss.

E[X]=\frac12,\qquad E[Y]=\frac12,\qquad E[X+Y]=1.

Does the identity still hold if Y=X, so the variables are completely dependent?

Yes: E[X+X]=2E[X]. Linearity does not require independence, provided the expectations exist. This is useful for adding waiting times in HW4.

These 0/1 variables are indicators: they mark whether an event occurs. Their expectations equal their event probabilities. For HW4, divide the total collection time into stages and add stage expectations without first proving stage independence. No full assignment derivation is needed here.

[Sources] - AIMA Appendix A.3 and Stanford CS229 probability review, Section 2.4. [/Sources]

Continuous Variables and a PDF

A continuous variable takes values along a range. Its probability density function (PDF) f_U(u) gives probability per unit length. Area gives probability.

Let U be uniform on [0,2]: equal-length intervals are equally likely. Its density is 1/2 on that interval and 0 outside it.

P(0.5\le U\le1)=\int_{0.5}^1 f_U(u)\,du=(1-0.5)\left(\frac12\right)=\frac14.

Here P(U=u)=0. Density can exceed 1. Only area gives probability.

The entire area is 2 times 1/2, which equals 1. A uniform density on [0,0.1] has height 10. A cumulative distribution function (CDF) gives F_U(u)=P(U<=u), accumulating area up to u. This prepares students for uniform angular positions in HW4.

[Sources] - AIMA Appendix A.3 and Stanford CS229 probability review, Sections 2.1-2.3. [/Sources]

A Joint Distribution

A joint distribution assigns probability to combinations of variable values.

Toy model: W is weather and U is umbrella use. These symbols are local to this model.

P(W=w,U=u) U=\text{Yes} U=\text{No}
W=\text{Sunny} 0.10 0.60
W=\text{Rainy} 0.20 0.10

All four entries are nonnegative and sum to 1.

Which entry represents rain and an umbrella? Which entries represent rain regardless of umbrella use?

Rain and umbrella: 0.20. Rain regardless of umbrella: 0.20+0.10=0.30.

A comma inside P(W=w,U=u) means the two events hold together. P(w,u) abbreviates the same thing once variables are clear. Every cell in a full joint table shares one total sum of 1. These numbers match the model at the start of Lecture 22.

Marginal and Conditional Distributions

Weather w P(w,U=\text{Yes}) P(w,U=\text{No})
Sunny 0.10 0.60
Rainy 0.20 0.10

Marginalization sums over a variable we do not keep:

P(W=\text{Rainy})=\sum_u P(W=\text{Rainy},U=u)=0.20+0.10=0.30.

Conditioning fixes a variable to its observed value:

P(W=\text{Rainy}\mid U=\text{Yes})=\frac{0.20}{0.10+0.20}=\frac23.

Ignoring umbrella use and observing an umbrella answer different questions. For continuous variables, marginalization integrates the joint density instead of summing it.

The marginal P(U=Yes)=0.3 provides the denominator. Conditioning keeps its Yes column and normalizes it. An unobserved variable in a marginal is not assumed to be No.

For continuous X,Y, the counterparts are f_X(x)=integral f_X,Y(x,y) dy and f_X|Y(x|y)=f_X,Y(x,y)/f_Y(y) when f_Y(y)>0. The weather variables in the table are discrete.

[Sources] - AIMA Section 12.3 and Appendix A.3. [/Sources]

The Chain Rule

The chain rule writes a joint probability as successive conditional probabilities. It requires no independence assumption.

For weather and umbrella use:

P(W=\text{Rainy},U=\text{Yes})=P(U=\text{Yes}\mid W=\text{Rainy})P(W=\text{Rainy}).

From our table: (2/3)(0.30)=0.20.

For three variables with values x,y,z:

P(x,y,z)=P(x)P(y\mid x)P(z\mid x,y).

If each variable is independent of all earlier variables together, the factors simplify to P(x,y,z)=P(x)P(y)P(z).

P(x,y,z) abbreviates P(X=x,Y=y,Z=z). Each conditional needs a positive-probability conditioning event in these discrete formulas. Zero-probability conditioning cases have zero joint mass and need a chosen version of the conditional.

For n variables, P(x_1,…,x_n)=product_i P(x_i|x_1,…,x_{i-1}). Pairwise independence alone does not imply the full product form. This is the basis of later Bayesian-network representations.

[Sources] - AIMA Section 12.2, product and chain rules, and Section 12.4, independence. [/Sources]

Bayes’ Rule and Its Vocabulary

Let H be a proposed explanation and E an observed event, with P(E)>0.

P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E)}.

Term Expression Meaning
Prior P(H) Belief before this observation
Likelihood P(E\mid H) Probability of this observation if H holds
Evidence probability P(E) Overall probability of this observation
Posterior P(H\mid E) Updated belief after the observation

P(E\mid H) and P(H\mid E) answer different questions. Bayes’ rule connects them using the prior and the evidence probability.

Likelihood here is evaluated at the observed event as a function of the hypothesis. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is correct. For a binary hypothesis, P(E)=P(E|H)P(H)+P(E|not H)P(not H). The next slide computes both terms.

Spam Given a Keyword

Let S mean spam and K mean an email contains a particular keyword. The toy model gives:

P(S)=0.30,\qquad P(K\mid S)=0.60,\qquad P(K\mid\neg S)=0.20.

The law of total probability adds the spam and non-spam routes:

P(K)=P(K\mid S)P(S)+P(K\mid\neg S)P(\neg S)=0.18+0.14=0.32.

P(S\mid K)=180/(180+140)=0.5625. The keyword raises the spam probability from 30\% to 56.25\%.

The frequencies describe a hypothetical population matching the probabilities, not an observed dataset. Out of 320 keyword emails, 180 are spam. This equals (0.60 times 0.30)/0.32. Both keyword-producing routes belong in the denominator.

Total probability sums over a disjoint, exhaustive partition. Here S and not S form that partition.

Rare Defects and False Positives

Defective: D. Flagged: F. Base rate: P(D)=0.01 (1% defective).

  • True-positive rate: flags 95% of defective parts, P(F\mid D)=0.95.
  • False-positive rate: flags 5% of working parts, P(F\mid\neg D)=0.05.

Among the flagged parts, what fraction are actually defective?

P(D\mid F)=95/(95+495)\approx16.1\%. Most flagged parts are working.

This uses the supplied false-positive example’s numbers in hypothetical parts inspection. A false positive is a positive prediction for an item without the condition. The flagged fraction is 0.95(0.01)+0.05(0.99)=0.059. The posterior is 0.0095/0.059, or 95/590.

The base rate changes the interpretation even if the conditional alarm rates stay fixed. Choosing a repair policy also requires repair and failure costs.

Joint-Probability Practice

An image classifier predicts a handwritten digit. Let:

  • X be image brightness, scaled to [0,1].
  • Y be the predicted digit, one of 0,1,\ldots,9.

Suppose P(Y=3)=0.15 and P(X>0.5\mid Y=3)=0.60.

What is the probability that an image is bright and predicted as digit 3?

P(X>0.5,Y=3)=P(X>0.5\mid Y=3)P(Y=3)=0.60(0.15)=0.09. This describes 9\% of images under the model.

X is continuous, but X>0.5 is an event with nonzero probability, so ordinary event probability applies. Y is the classifier’s prediction, not the true digit. The calculation therefore does not measure accuracy.

Takeaways

  • Define the sample space, events, and variables before calculating.
  • Conditioning keeps the observed event and rescales its probabilities.
  • Independence is a property of the model, not a default assumption.
  • PMFs assign mass to values. PDFs give probabilities through area.
  • Bayes’ rule combines likelihoods and base rates to update beliefs.
  • Linearity lets us add expectations without assuming independence.
